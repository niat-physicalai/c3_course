# Part 1 — From Physical Quantities to Meaningful Readings

## Measuring the World

### The Chain from Quantity to Decision

Every sensor system has the same underlying chain, and it is worth naming each link, because problems can arise at any of them:

```text
Physical quantity  →  Sensor  →  Signal  →  Raw reading  →  Measurement  →  Decision
 (temperature)     (thermistor) (voltage)   (a number)    (in °C, with      (fan on)
                                                           limits)
```

- The **physical quantity** is the real condition: how warm the air really is.
- The **sensor** responds to it physically. A thermistor changes its resistance, and a light-dependent resistor changes with light.
- The **signal** is the electrical form of that response: a voltage, a stream of pulses or a digital message.
- The **raw reading** is the number your program receives after the signal is converted: an ADC value, a count, or a value from a library.
- The **measurement** is the raw reading converted into a meaningful quantity, such as degrees Celsius, together with an understanding of how far to trust it.
- The **decision** is what your program does with that information.

### Signal, Reading, Measurement

Here is the most important idea in this reading. There are **three different things** that people casually call "the sensor value", and confusing them is the source of many mistakes:

| Level | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Sensor signal** | The electrical output of the sensor | 1.8 V on a wire |
| **Raw reading** | The number the microcontroller gets | 28,400 from the ADC |
| **Calibrated physical measurement** | A quantity in real units, with known accuracy and limits | 23 °C, plus or minus 2 °C |

A raw reading only becomes a *measurement* when you know how the sensor's signal relates to the physical quantity, and have checked that relationship against something trustworthy. That process of establishing and checking the relationship is **calibration**.

Some sensors do much of this work for you. The DHT11, for example, is calibrated by its manufacturer, so the library hands you numbers already in degrees Celsius and percent. Others, like an LDR in a voltage divider, give you only a raw number whose relationship to real light levels you would have to work out yourself. You will meet both kinds in this reading.

### Which Interface Does the Sensor Use?

To read a sensor, you first need to know how it delivers its information. You have already met the first two of these:

| Sensor type | How it delivers information | How you read it | Examples in this reading |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Simple digital** | HIGH or LOW on a pin | A digital input | Buttons and some sensor modules |
| **Analog** | A voltage that varies smoothly | The ADC, through an analog input | The LDR in a voltage divider |
| **Digital data** | Numbers sent as a message on a wire | A library that decodes the message | The DHT11 |
| **Streamed audio** | A fast stream of sensor samples | A dedicated hardware block and library | The PDM microphone |

The last two are new. In both, the sensor contains its own electronics and the microcontroller receives the result through a specialised interface. The decoding details for those interfaces belong to later modules. Here, you use a library that handles them, and concentrate on what the numbers mean.

### Matching Sensors to Quantities

| Physical quantity | Typical sensing approach | Interface |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature | Thermistor (resistance changes with temperature), or a temperature sensor chip | Analog, or digital data |
| Humidity | A sensing element whose electrical properties change with moisture | Usually digital data |
| Light | LDR (resistance falls as light rises), or a light-sensor chip | Analog, or digital data |
| Sound | Microphone | Analog, or a digital stream such as PDM |

Choosing between them is an engineering decision, as you saw earlier: What range and accuracy do you need? How fast does it change? How much does it cost, and what does it require of your hardware? A simple LDR is cheap and easy to read, but gives only a relative light level. A digital light-sensor chip can report calibrated values but is more complex to use.

# Part 2 — The Sensors

## Temperature and Humidity: The DHT11

### What It Measures, and How

The **DHT11** is a widely used, low-cost sensor that reports both temperature and humidity. According to the manufacturer's datasheet, it combines a **resistive humidity-sensing component** and an **NTC temperature-sensing component** (a thermistor whose resistance falls as temperature rises) with a small built-in microcontroller. That microcontroller converts the measurements, using calibration coefficients stored inside the sensor at the factory, and sends the results to your board as a digital message.

That last point puts the DHT11 in the "digital data" category. You do not read a voltage from it. You ask it for a reading, and it answers with numbers.

**What is relative humidity?** Air can hold only a limited amount of water vapour, and warmer air can hold more. **Relative humidity (RH)** is the amount of water vapour in the air, expressed as a percentage of the most it could hold at that temperature. At 100% RH, the air is saturated. Because the maximum depends on temperature, the same amount of moisture gives a different relative humidity at different temperatures. That is why temperature and humidity are so often measured together.

### Ranges, Accuracy and Limits

Here are the main figures from the DHT11's datasheet. These are the sensor's *stated limits*, and they matter as much as the readings:

| Property | Stated by the datasheet |
|---|---|
| Temperature measurement range | 0 to 50 °C |
| Temperature accuracy | About ±1 °C typical, ±2 °C maximum |
| Humidity measurement range | 20 to 90% RH at 25 °C (the usable range narrows at temperature extremes) |
| Humidity accuracy | ±4% RH at 25 °C, ±5% RH over 0 to 50 °C |
| Resolution | 1 °C and 1% RH |
| Response time (63% of a step change) | Humidity about 10 s typical (6 to 15 s); temperature up to 30 s |
| Supply voltage | 3 to 5.5 V |
| Minimum time between readings | 1 second |
| After power-up | Do not send instructions for the first second |

> **Assumption.** These figures come from a translated copy of the manufacturer's datasheet distributed through an electronics distributor. Some suppliers quote slightly different accuracy figures for the same part. If they disagree, use the datasheet that came with *your* sensor or module, and treat the least favourable figure as your working limit.

Read the table again as an engineer. What does it tell you about how to use the readings?

- **Resolution of 1 °C.** The sensor reports whole degrees. It cannot show that the temperature changed by half a degree.
- **Accuracy of ±2 °C.** A reading of 25 °C could correspond to a real temperature anywhere from about 23 to 27 °C. Two DHT11s side by side may disagree by a degree or two, and neither is "wrong".
- **Response time.** After a sudden change, the sensor takes many seconds to catch up. Do not expect it to react instantly.
- **Range.** Readings outside 0 to 50 °C or the humidity range are unreliable, and exposure beyond the range can shift later readings by up to about 3% RH for a while, according to the datasheet.

Two further cautions from the datasheet are worth remembering. Because humidity depends on temperature, the sensor should be mounted **away from parts that generate heat**, and it should be kept away from condensation and strong sunlight.

### Connecting the DHT11

A DHT11 has three connections that matter: **power (VCC)**, **ground (GND)** and **data**. The data line is a single wire, and that single wire is open-drain in nature: the sensor pulls it low, and a **pull-up resistor** returns it high. You already know why a pull-up is needed. Many DHT11 *modules* have the pull-up resistor (and a small filtering capacitor) already fitted on their small circuit board, and a bare sensor needs one added. Adafruit's guide suggests 10 kΩ, and the datasheet recommends about 5 kΩ for short cables. Check what your part includes.

Wire it in this order, with the board unpowered:

1. **Disconnect USB.**
2. **Connect the sensor's ground** to a GND pin.
3. **Connect the sensor's power** to the board's **3.3 V** pin. The DHT11 works from 3 to 5.5 V, and using 3.3 V keeps the data line within the safe range for the ESP32-S3.
4. **Connect the data pin** to the GPIO pin you chose.
5. **Check the pull-up** is present and goes to 3.3 V.
6. **Reconnect USB.**

> **Caution about voltages.** If a module is powered from 5 V and has a pull-up connected to that 5 V supply, its data line can swing up to 5 V, which is above what the ESP32-S3's input pins are specified for. When in doubt, power the module from 3.3 V. Adafruit notes that some setups have worked better with 5 V power while keeping the pull-up resistor connected to 3.3 V. Whichever you choose, the pull-up must go to 3.3 V, and you should consult your module's documentation.

### Reading It in CircuitPython

The `adafruit_dht` library decodes the sensor's message for you. Copy it to the `lib` folder of your board, as directed by your course setup, then use:

```python
import time
import board
import adafruit_dht

dht = adafruit_dht.DHT11(board.CHOSEN_DHT_PIN)     # replace with your data pin

while True:
    try:
        temperature = dht.temperature     # degrees Celsius
        humidity = dht.humidity           # percent relative humidity
        print("Temperature:", temperature, "C   Humidity:", humidity, "%")
    except RuntimeError as error:
        print("Read failed:", error.args[0])

    time.sleep(2)
```

Here is what the code does:

- `adafruit_dht.DHT11(pin)` creates an object for the sensor on that pin.
- `dht.temperature` and `dht.humidity` ask the sensor for a reading, and return numbers already in °C and %RH.
- The `try` / `except RuntimeError` block is essential. The library raises a `RuntimeError` when a reading fails, for example because of a checksum error or too little data. Adafruit's example code notes that such errors are fairly common with these sensors, and the right response is simply to try again. Without the `try`, one failed read would stop your program.
- `time.sleep(2)` keeps the reading rate well within the sensor's minimum of one second per reading.

> **Assumption: check that the library works on your board.** The library uses a CircuitPython feature called `pulseio` to time the sensor's pulses. In the REPL, run `import pulseio` and `import adafruit_dht`. If either fails, or the program repeatedly reports that it cannot find the sensor, the library may not be available for your board and version, and you should ask your instructor. If the program says the sensor was not found, check the wiring, power, pull-up resistor and pin choice before suspecting the software.

> **Try it: Predict, observe, explain.**
> 1. **Predict.** What temperature do you expect the room to show? What humidity?
> 2. **Run** the program and record several readings. Do they stay steady? Do you see any failed reads?
> 3. **Breathe gently** near the sensor for a few seconds. What happens to the humidity reading, and how quickly? Then stop. How long does it take to return? Compare that with the response times in the datasheet.
> 4. **Warm the sensor** by holding a warm hand near it (not touching it). Watch how slowly the temperature responds.
> 5. **Explain.** What do your observations tell you about how to interpret a reading, and about placing the sensor?

### Reading It Sensibly

Because of the sensor's limits, some habits are wise:

- **Do not read faster than the sensor can measure.** More reads will not give more information, and reading too often can cause errors.
- **Keep the last good value.** If a read fails, do not treat it as "temperature zero". Keep using the previous valid reading until the next one succeeds.
- **Do not over-interpret small changes.** With 1 °C resolution and ±2 °C accuracy, a change of one degree is not strong evidence of anything.
- **Use thresholds with a margin.** A limit of 30 °C is a decision about a reading, not a precise physical event.

## Light: The LDR

### How the LDR Senses Light

You met the **light-dependent resistor** in the first module. Its resistance *decreases* as the light falling on it *increases*. Because a microcontroller reads voltages, not resistances, an LDR is used in a **voltage divider** with a fixed resistor, so that its changing resistance becomes a changing voltage:

```text
 3.3 V ───┬───
          │
        [ LDR ]              ← resistance changes with light
          │
          ├────────── to the analog input pin
          │
        [ fixed resistor ]   ← for example 10 kΩ
          │
 GND ─────┴───
```

The voltage at the junction is then converted by the ADC, exactly as with the potentiometer. Nothing about the reading process is new. What is new is the *interpretation*.

> **Assumption.** LDRs vary considerably in their resistance in light and dark, so the fixed resistor value that works best depends on your part. Check its datasheet, and start with a mid-range value such as 10 kΩ. And which direction the reading moves as light increases depends on the divider: if the LDR is on the upper side, as in the figure, the voltage rises with more light. If you swap the two, it falls. Do not assume. Test it.

### A Raw Number, Not a Light Level

Here is the LDR reading program:

```python
import time
import board
import analogio

ldr = analogio.AnalogIn(board.CHOSEN_LDR_PIN)     # an analog-capable pin

while True:
    print(ldr.value)
    time.sleep(0.2)
```

Run it, and you will see a number that changes as you cover the sensor or shine a light on it. But what *is* that number? It is a raw ADC reading of a voltage that depends on the LDR's resistance, the fixed resistor, the supply voltage and the ADC's own behaviour. It is not a measurement in lux (the unit of illuminance). Converting to lux would need the LDR's characteristic curve (which is non-linear and differs between parts) and a reference light meter to check it. The number tells you **whether it is brighter or darker, and roughly by how much**, which is often all you need.

That is what we mean by a **relative** reading. It is meaningful for comparisons ("brighter than a minute ago", "darker than my threshold") but not for absolute claims ("the light level is 300 lux").

### Characterising Your LDR

A good habit, and the practical route to a useful threshold, is to **characterise** the sensor: record what it reads under conditions you can reproduce.

> **Try it: Build a table of readings.** With the LDR program running, record the raw reading under each condition:
>
> | Condition | Raw reading |
> |---|---|
> | Sensor covered completely (a cupped hand or a small box) | |
> | Normal room lighting | |
> | A shadow across the sensor | |
> | A phone torch shining on it | |
> | Sensor facing a window in daylight | |
>
> Then answer:
> 1. Which direction does the reading move as the light increases?
> 2. How far apart are "dark" and "bright"? Is there a clear gap, or do the ranges overlap?
> 3. If you were choosing a threshold between "dark" and "bright", where would it go, and why there?
> 4. Do readings drift when the light seems steady? What could explain it?

### From Raw Reading to a Relative Scale

Once you know your sensor's darkest and brightest readings, you can convert to a simple **0 to 100 scale**. This is a two-point calibration of a relative scale:

```python
DARK_READING = 4000        # example values: use the readings you recorded
BRIGHT_READING = 52000

def light_percent(raw):
    fraction = (raw - DARK_READING) / (BRIGHT_READING - DARK_READING)
    fraction = max(0.0, min(1.0, fraction))       # keep between 0 and 1
    return fraction * 100
```

Now "20" means "toward the dark end of what this sensor sees in this setup". It is much easier to reason about, and to set thresholds on, than a raw number. But it is still a *relative* scale: it says nothing about lux.

### Things That Trip Up Light Sensing

- **The system's own light.** If an LED lamp that your program controls shines onto the LDR, then turning the lamp on changes the reading, which may change the decision that turned it on. You saw this problem in the first module's street-light example. Place the LDR so that it sees the room, not the lamp.
- **A shadow from your own hand or body.** Readings change when you stand near the sensor.
- **Flicker.** Artificial lights and screens can flicker, and readings taken at different instants may differ. Averaging helps.
- **Response is not instant.** LDRs take some time to respond to a change, and the exact time depends on the part.
- **Near the threshold.** Just as with the potentiometer, a single threshold near a wobbling reading causes flicker in the output. Use two thresholds (hysteresis).

## Sound: The PDM Microphone

### Measuring Sound

Sound is a rapid variation in air pressure. A **microphone** converts those pressure variations into an electrical signal. A louder sound makes larger variations. But sound differs from the slowly changing quantities we have looked at, in one important way: **the information is in the fast changes.** A potentiometer position or a room's temperature can be read a few times a second. Sound varies thousands of times a second, so a single reading tells you almost nothing. You need a *rapid series of samples*, and you interpret the whole series.

Recall the intuition about sampling from the previous reading: taking photographs quickly enough to capture the motion. For sound, "quickly enough" means many thousands of samples per second. For speech-quality audio, a common choice is 16,000 samples per second.

### What "PDM" Means

A **PDM microphone** is a digital microphone. Instead of producing a varying voltage that you read with an ADC, it contains its own converter and outputs a **very fast stream of one-bit values**. Where the sound pressure is high, more of the bits in the stream are 1s. Where it is low, more are 0s. The *density* of ones follows the sound wave, which is where the name **pulse-density modulation** comes from.

```text
Sound pressure:         high ▲                  low ▲
Bit stream:             1 1 0 1 1 1 0 1 1        0 0 1 0 0 0 1 0 0
                        (mostly ones)            (mostly zeros)
```

The microcontroller supplies a fast **clock signal** to the microphone, and receives the bit stream on a **data** pin. Special hardware and software then filter the stream into ordinary sample numbers. You do not read a PDM microphone with a digital input or with the ADC. It needs its own interface.

### Check Support Before You Use It

Whether you can use a PDM microphone with CircuitPython on your board depends on two things:

- **Hardware.** You need a PDM microphone, either built into your board or wired to it, and you need to know which pins carry its clock and data.
- **Software.** CircuitPython provides PDM input through the `audiobusio` module (`audiobusio.PDMIn`). Its support has varied by chip and by version. CircuitPython's documentation pages for several versions list PDM input as not available on Espressif chips, while the project's issue tracker records that it has been implemented for Espressif chips since CircuitPython 9.2.2, working on the ESP32-S3 but not on the ESP32-S2.

Documentation and behaviour can therefore differ, and the honest approach is to **test on your board**. This short check uses only the documented interface:

```python
import board
import audiobusio

MIC_CLOCK = board.CHOSEN_MIC_CLOCK_PIN     # replace with the pins from your board's documentation
MIC_DATA = board.CHOSEN_MIC_DATA_PIN

try:
    mic = audiobusio.PDMIn(MIC_CLOCK, MIC_DATA, sample_rate=16000, bit_depth=16)
    print("PDM input is available")
    mic.deinit()
except NotImplementedError:
    print("PDM input is not supported by this CircuitPython build")
```

> **Assumption.** If your board has a built-in microphone, its board definition may provide pin names for it (for example, names that include `MICROPHONE`). Check `dir(board)` and your board's documentation. If your hardware does not include a PDM microphone, or the check reports that it is not supported, skip the rest of this section. The remainder of the reading does not depend on it.

### Reading Loudness (Where Supported)

Suppose the check succeeds. The `record` method fills a buffer with samples. The program below measures **relative loudness** as the RMS (root mean square) size of the variations, after removing the constant offset the samples sit on:

```python
import array
import math
import board
import audiobusio

MIC_CLOCK = board.CHOSEN_MIC_CLOCK_PIN
MIC_DATA = board.CHOSEN_MIC_DATA_PIN

samples = array.array("H", [0] * 160)          # 160 unsigned 16-bit samples (10 ms at 16 kHz)

def loudness(buf):
    mean = sum(buf) / len(buf)                 # the constant offset
    total = 0
    for s in buf:
        difference = s - mean
        total = total + difference * difference
    return math.sqrt(total / len(buf))         # RMS of the variations

mic = audiobusio.PDMIn(MIC_CLOCK, MIC_DATA, sample_rate=16000, bit_depth=16)

while True:
    mic.record(samples, len(samples))          # blocks while recording
    print(loudness(samples))
```

Here is what it does:

- `array.array("H", ...)` creates a buffer of unsigned 16-bit numbers to hold the samples.
- `mic.record(samples, len(samples))` fills the buffer with about 10 milliseconds of sound. The documentation notes that recording *blocks*, which means the program waits until it finishes.
- `loudness()` first subtracts the average (the sound wave swings above and below a constant level), and then finds the typical size of the swings. A louder sound gives a larger number.

> **This is still a raw, relative measurement.** The number is a measure of loudness *in the microphone's own arbitrary units*. It is not decibels. Turning it into a sound level in dB would need a calibration against a reference sound meter. What you *can* do reliably is compare: quiet against talking, talking against a clap.

> **Try it: Explore sound (where supported).** Record the loudness in a quiet room (the "noise floor"), while speaking normally, while clapping and with a phone playing a steady tone at different distances. Then decide: where would you put a threshold for "someone is making noise"? How far above the quiet reading must it be to avoid false triggers?

If a microphone is not available, a **sound sensor module** with a digital threshold output (read like any digital sensor) or an analog output (read with the ADC) can serve a similar purpose. Check its documentation. Such modules typically tell you only whether the sound is above a threshold, or give an envelope-like level, rather than the raw sound.

# Part 3 — Making Sense of Sensor Readings

## From Raw Readings to Useful Information

### Raw and Calibrated, Side by Side

Here is where each of this reading's sensors stands on the road from signal to measurement:

| Sensor | What your program receives | Is it a calibrated measurement? | What it can honestly tell you |
|---|---|---|---|
| **DHT11** | Temperature in °C and humidity in %RH, from the library | Calibrated by the manufacturer, within stated limits (±2 °C, ±5% RH, 1-unit resolution) | Approximate temperature and humidity, and clear trends over seconds to minutes |
| **LDR in a divider** | A raw ADC number | No | Brighter or darker, and roughly by how much (relative) |
| **PDM microphone** | Samples, then a loudness number you computed | No | Louder or quieter (relative), not decibels |
| **Potentiometer** (from earlier) | A raw ADC number | No | Knob position, roughly |

The safe rule is: **never present a number as a physical measurement unless you know how it was calibrated and what its limits are.**

### Calibration

**Calibration** means comparing a sensor's output with a trusted reference, and using the comparison to correct or scale the readings. A common, simple method is a **two-point calibration**: measure the sensor at two known conditions, and use them to define a straight-line correction.

Suppose a temperature sensor reads 21 when a trusted reference thermometer reads 20 °C (a cool place), and reads 44 when the reference reads 40 °C (a warm place). (These are example values.) You can define the corrected value as:

```python
def calibrate(raw, raw_low, raw_high, ref_low, ref_high):
    """Two-point calibration: map raw readings onto the reference scale."""
    fraction = (raw - raw_low) / (raw_high - raw_low)
    return ref_low + fraction * (ref_high - ref_low)

corrected = calibrate(30, raw_low=21, raw_high=44, ref_low=20, ref_high=40)
```

A raw reading of 30 corrects to about 27.8 °C. The method is the same as the two-point relative scale you built for the LDR. The only difference is that here the endpoints are *trusted reference values*, and so the result is a genuine measurement.

Three cautions about calibration:

- **It is only as good as your reference.** A poor reference thermometer gives a poor calibration.
- **It corrects only the kind of error you modelled.** A straight-line correction will not fix a sensor that is non-linear, or that varies with humidity.
- **It does not remove noise or limits.** A sensor with 1 °C resolution still has 1 °C resolution after calibration.

Not every application needs calibration. If your program only asks, "Is it darker than a minute ago?", a relative scale is enough. Calibrate when the actual value matters, for example when a real limit must be respected.

### Variation, Noise and the Environment

Sensor readings vary. Some variation is **noise**, small random fluctuation in the measurement chain. Some comes from the **environment**, the world the sensor is actually in. Both are normal, and a good engineer expects them.

Sources of variation include:

- **Electrical noise** in the wiring and the ADC, as you saw with the potentiometer.
- **Placement.** A temperature sensor next to a warm chip, in sunlight or in a draught measures its surroundings, not the room.
- **Slow response.** The DHT11 and LDR take time to settle, so a reading taken immediately after a change reflects a mixture of old and new conditions.
- **Local effects.** Your breath, hand or body near the sensor, or shadows on the LDR.
- **Sensor-to-sensor differences.** Two sensors of the same type can read differently.
- **The system itself.** Heat from a running controller, or light from your own LED.

Practical ways to deal with variation:

- **Average several readings** to reduce random noise, as you did with the potentiometer.
- **Reject bad readings.** If a value is impossible or fails a check, keep the last good one instead of using it.
- **Place sensors thoughtfully**, away from heat sources, direct sunlight and your own outputs.
- **Use margins and hysteresis** in your thresholds, so ordinary wobble does not cause flicker.
- **Test under different conditions** and record what you see. Understanding how *your* sensor behaves is worth more than trusting a specification alone.

> **Try it: Compare readings under different conditions.** Record the readings of each sensor you have, under at least three different physical conditions each: for example, the temperature near a window versus away from it, the LDR in a shadow versus in light, humidity before and after breathing on the sensor. For each sensor, note how large the *real* change is compared with the *noise* you see when nothing is changing. Which of your conditions produced changes that are clearly larger than the noise?

### Sampling Sensor Data

**Sampling** means taking readings at intervals. How often should you sample? It depends on how fast the quantity changes, and what the sensor can deliver:

| Quantity or sensor | How fast it changes | A sensible sampling approach |
|---|---|---|
| Room temperature | Slowly (minutes) | Every few seconds is plenty. The DHT11 requires at least one second between reads |
| Humidity | Slowly, but breath can change it in seconds | Every few seconds |
| Light (LDR) | Can change abruptly (a light switched on) | Several times a second |
| Sound | Thousands of times a second | Many thousands of samples per second, in short bursts |

There are two errors to avoid. **Sampling too slowly** misses changes you care about. **Sampling too fast** wastes effort, can overload the sensor (the DHT11 gives errors if read too quickly) and produces data that is mostly repetition. Match the rate to the physics of the quantity and the limits of the sensor.

This has a consequence for programs with several sensors: **each sensor may need a different sampling rate.** We return to this in the integration part.

### Comparing Readings from Different Sensors

It is tempting to compare sensors directly, but their raw numbers are not comparable. A temperature of 25, a humidity of 55, an LDR reading of 30,000 and a loudness of 900 are on completely different scales and in different units. To combine them, you first need a common language. Two approaches are common:

- **Turn each reading into a condition.** Compare each with its own threshold, and get yes-or-no answers: "too warm", "too humid", "dark", "noisy". Then combine the conditions with logic. This is the most common approach, and it is the one used in the integrated system that follows.
- **Scale each reading to a common range**, such as 0 to 1 or 0 to 100, then compare or combine them numerically. This suits situations where you want a combined "score".

Either way, the conversion for each sensor is *sensor-specific*, because each sensor has its own units, range and behaviour. There is no universal conversion.

# Part 4 — Integration

## Combining Sensors and Outputs

You now have sensors that read temperature, humidity, light and (perhaps) sound, and outputs that switch LEDs and buzzers. Real systems combine several of each. That raises new questions about how to structure the program, how to combine conditions and how to make several things happen on different schedules.

### Sensor → Processing → Actuator, Revisited

The loop you know, Sense → Compute → Actuate, has more structure inside it once sensors multiply:

```text
SENSE                    PROCESS                            DECIDE            ACT
─────                    ───────                            ──────            ───
LDR (analog)     ─►  average, convert to relative   ─►  is it dark?      ─►  lamp LED
DHT11 (data)     ─►  check validity, keep last good ─►  too warm? humid? ─►  warning LED, buzzer
Button (digital) ─►  debounce                       ─►  muted?          ─►  (changes the buzzer's behaviour)
```

Three things stand out.

1. Each input has its own **processing**: filtering, validating, converting.
2. The **decisions** come from the processed values, and may combine several of them.
3. The **actions** follow from the decisions. Keeping "decide" separate from "act" makes the program much easier to understand and change.

This is the same discipline you used earlier when you kept "decide which zone applies" apart from "act on the zone".

### Making Decisions from More Than One Condition

With several conditions, your program combines them with logic:

- **AND**: all conditions must be true. "It is dark *and* someone is present."
- **OR**: at least one must be true. "It is too hot *or* too humid."
- **NOT**: the opposite. "The buzzer is *not* muted."
- **Priority**: when several conditions hold, the most important one wins. "A fault overrides a warning."

A **decision table** keeps this organised. Here is one for a small room monitor:

| Dark? | Too warm or too humid? | Muted? | Lamp LED | Warning LED | Buzzer |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| No | No | Either | Off | Off | Silent |
| Yes | No | Either | On | Off | Silent |
| Either | Yes | No | (as dark) | Blinking | Beeping |
| Either | Yes | Yes | (as dark) | Blinking | Silent |

Every row is a situation, and every output has a clear answer in every situation. If a combination is missing from your table, your program has a gap that you will discover later, at the worst moment.

Each condition should be **stable**: use hysteresis for "dark", margins for temperature and humidity, and a debounced button. Otherwise, a noisy reading makes the outputs flicker.

### Coordinating Timing

Different parts of the system run on different clocks:

- The LDR can be read many times a second.
- The DHT11 must be read at most about once per second, and every few seconds is plenty.
- A blinking LED or beeping buzzer needs precise timing.
- The button must be checked often to catch presses.

If you write `time.sleep(2)` after reading the DHT11, then for two seconds the program cannot read the button, update the LED or notice anything else. The solution is the one you used in the debouncing reading: **do not stop the program to wait, keep a clock and act when the time comes.** A useful pattern is to give each slow task its own "last time" variable:

```python
last_dht_read = -100          # so the first read happens immediately
DHT_INTERVAL = 3              # seconds between DHT11 reads

while True:
    now = time.monotonic()

    if now - last_dht_read >= DHT_INTERVAL:
        last_dht_read = now
        # ... read the DHT11 here ...

    # ... everything else runs every time round the loop ...
```

The loop runs continuously, and the DHT11 is read only when its interval has elapsed. Blinking works the same way: a time-based expression such as `int(now * 2) % 2 == 0` is true for half a second, then false for half a second, without any `sleep`.

## Building an Integrated System: A Room Monitor

Now we put it all together. This is an **example design**, and you should adapt it to the sensors and outputs in your kit.

**The requirements:**

- A **lamp LED** turns on when the room gets dark (LDR).
- A **warning LED** blinks and a **buzzer** beeps when the room is too warm or too humid (DHT11).
- A **button** mutes the buzzer, and pressing it again un-mutes it.

**Sensors and outputs used:** an analog input (LDR), a digital-data sensor (DHT11), a digital input (button), and three digital outputs (two LEDs and a buzzer). That includes analog input, digital input and digital data, all working together.

> **Assumption.** The code assumes: your LDR reading *rises* as light increases (check yours, and flip the comparisons if the opposite); the buzzer is an active buzzer suited to a GPIO pin, as described earlier; and the `StableInput` class from the previous reading on reliable inputs is available. It is repeated in the program below so that everything is in one place. All threshold values are **example values** for you to replace with your own measurements.

```python
import time
import board
import digitalio
import analogio
import adafruit_dht

# ---------- A debounced input (from the reliable-input reading) ----------
class StableInput:
    def __init__(self, pin, settle_time=0.03):
        self.pin = pin
        self.settle_time = settle_time
        self.stable = pin.value
        self.changed = False
        self._last_raw = self.stable
        self._changed_at = time.monotonic()

    def update(self):
        raw = self.pin.value
        now = time.monotonic()
        self.changed = False
        if raw != self._last_raw:
            self._last_raw = raw
            self._changed_at = now
        if raw != self.stable and now - self._changed_at >= self.settle_time:
            self.stable = raw
            self.changed = True
        return self.stable

def read_average(pin, samples=8):
    total = 0
    for _ in range(samples):
        total = total + pin.value
    return total // samples

# ---------- Hardware setup (replace every CHOSEN_ name) ----------
lamp = digitalio.DigitalInOut(board.CHOSEN_LAMP_PIN)
lamp.direction = digitalio.Direction.OUTPUT

warning = digitalio.DigitalInOut(board.CHOSEN_WARNING_PIN)
warning.direction = digitalio.Direction.OUTPUT

buzzer = digitalio.DigitalInOut(board.CHOSEN_BUZZER_PIN)
buzzer.direction = digitalio.Direction.OUTPUT

ldr = analogio.AnalogIn(board.CHOSEN_LDR_PIN)
dht = adafruit_dht.DHT11(board.CHOSEN_DHT_PIN)

button_pin = digitalio.DigitalInOut(board.CHOSEN_BUTTON_PIN)
button_pin.direction = digitalio.Direction.INPUT
button_pin.pull = digitalio.Pull.UP
mute_button = StableInput(button_pin)

# ---------- Settings (example values: replace with your measurements) ----------
DARK_BELOW = 20000           # go "dark" when the light reading falls below this
BRIGHT_ABOVE = 26000         # go "bright" only when it rises above this (hysteresis)
TEMP_LIMIT_C = 30            # too warm at or above this
HUMIDITY_LIMIT = 70          # too humid at or above this
DHT_INTERVAL = 3             # seconds between DHT11 reads

# ---------- State the program remembers ----------
is_dark = False
temperature = None
humidity = None
muted = False
last_dht_read = -DHT_INTERVAL

while True:
    now = time.monotonic()

    # ===== SENSE and PROCESS =====
    light = read_average(ldr)

    if not is_dark and light < DARK_BELOW:
        is_dark = True
    elif is_dark and light > BRIGHT_ABOVE:
        is_dark = False

    if now - last_dht_read >= DHT_INTERVAL:
        last_dht_read = now
        try:
            new_temperature = dht.temperature
            new_humidity = dht.humidity
            if new_temperature is not None and new_humidity is not None:
                temperature = new_temperature       # keep only valid readings
                humidity = new_humidity
        except RuntimeError:
            pass                                    # a failed read: keep the last good values

    mute_button.update()
    if mute_button.changed and not mute_button.stable:    # a clean press (active-low)
        muted = not muted

    # ===== DECIDE =====
    too_warm = temperature is not None and temperature >= TEMP_LIMIT_C
    too_humid = humidity is not None and humidity >= HUMIDITY_LIMIT
    alert = too_warm or too_humid

    blink_on = int(now * 2) % 2 == 0              # true for half a second, then false

    # ===== ACT =====
    lamp.value = is_dark
    warning.value = alert and blink_on
    buzzer.value = alert and blink_on and not muted

    time.sleep(0.02)
```

Study how it is organised, because the structure matters as much as the details:

- **Setup** creates every input and output once.
- **Settings** hold every threshold in one place, so changing behaviour means changing a number.
- **State** variables (`is_dark`, `muted`, `temperature`, `humidity`) hold what the program *remembers* between loops.
- The main loop has three clearly separated parts: **sense and process**, **decide** and **act**.
- **Hysteresis** stabilises the darkness decision, a **debounced** input handles the button and the **last good** DHT11 values are kept when a read fails.
- The short `time.sleep(0.02)` keeps the loop from running needlessly fast. Nothing in the program waits for long, so every task stays responsive.

> **Try it: Predict, then modify.**
> 1. **Predict.** For each row of the decision table, predict what each output will do. Then check with a real test: cover the LDR, warm the DHT11 with your breath and press the button.
> 2. **Change a threshold.** Lower `TEMP_LIMIT_C` to just above the current room temperature. What happens? Raise it far above. What happens?
> 3. **Change the logic.** Make the buzzer sound only when the room is *both* too warm *and* too humid, while the warning LED still lights for either. Which lines change?
> 4. **Add an input.** If you have a working microphone, add a "noisy" condition that also lights the warning LED. Where does the new sensing code go, and where does the new decision go?
> 5. **Break it deliberately.** Disconnect the DHT11's data wire while the program runs. What happens? Is that what you wanted the system to do? What could you add to make the failure visible?

## When an Integrated System Misbehaves

With several sensors and outputs, a problem can hide in many places. The key skill is to **separate hardware problems from software and logic problems**, and to test each part on its own.

### A Systematic Approach

Work from the outside in, and test the smallest piece you can:

```text
Power & wiring ─► Each sensor alone ─► Each output alone ─► Logic with known inputs ─► Timing ─► The whole system
```

1. **Power and wiring.** Is everything powered, with a shared ground? Are there loose connections? Are the sensors on the right voltage?
2. **Each sensor alone.** Write a tiny program that reads *only* that sensor and prints the raw values. Do they look sensible? Do they change when the physical condition changes? If not, the problem is in the sensor, wiring or reading code, not in the decision logic.
3. **Each output alone.** Write a tiny program that turns each output on and off. If the LED does not light here, the fault is in the wiring or component, not the logic.
4. **The logic with known inputs.** Temporarily replace the sensor readings with fixed test values, and see whether the outputs do what your decision table says:

    ```python
    temperature = 35          # test value: pretend it is hot
    humidity = 40
    ```

    If the logic behaves correctly with test values but not with real readings, the fault is in sensing. If it behaves wrongly even with known values, the fault is in the logic itself.
5. **Timing.** Does something in the loop wait too long? Are the sensors being read at sensible rates?
6. **The whole system.** Only when the pieces work alone, combine them.

Printing the internal values is your window into what the program believes. A line such as `print(light, temperature, humidity, is_dark, muted)` shows both the inputs and the state, and often identifies the problem at once.

### Common Symptoms

| Symptom | Likely cause | First thing to check |
|---|---|---|
| DHT11 always reports "sensor not found" or fails | Wiring, power, missing pull-up, wrong pin, library or `pulseio` support | Wiring and pull-up first. Then `import pulseio` and the library in the REPL |
| DHT11 values freeze | Reads failing silently and old values being kept | Print when a read fails, and print the time of the last good read |
| Temperature seems wrong by a degree or two | Within the sensor's stated accuracy, placement effects | Compare with a reference thermometer. Check the datasheet limits. Move the sensor away from heat |
| Humidity jumps when you approach | Your breath or body, not a fault | Keep away, and watch the response time |
| LDR reading moves the opposite way to what you expected | The divider is wired the other way round | Test with a torch and flip the comparison |
| Lamp flickers near dusk | Single threshold, noise, or the lamp lighting the LDR | Add hysteresis. Reposition the LDR |
| Buzzer sounds when it should be muted | Button not debounced, or the `muted` logic is wrong | Print `muted` and the button events |
| Whole system feels slow or misses button presses | A long `sleep`, or a blocking read | Look for waits in the loop. Use timers instead |
| Outputs do the opposite of the logic | Active-low wiring or module | Test each output alone |
| Everything worked, then a new sensor broke the rest | Two things sharing a pin, a supply drooping under load, or a wiring slip | Recheck pins and power. Add parts one at a time |

> **Assumption.** Any PDM microphone code must also fit the timing of the loop. Recording blocks while it fills its buffer, so keep the buffer small (for example about 10 milliseconds of sound), and check that your other tasks still run smoothly.
