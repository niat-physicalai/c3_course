# Part 1 — The Digital World

## Physical States and Their Digital Representation

Look around you and notice how many things have two states. A light is on or off. A door is open or closed. A switch is up or down. A button is pressed or not pressed. A tank is full or not full.

These are **physical states**: real conditions in the physical world. A microcontroller cannot look at a door. It can only work with **electrical signals**. To use a physical state in a program, we need a way to *represent* it, as an electrical signal that a chip can measure and a program can use.

That representation is what makes a signal **digital**. In its simplest form:

- The physical world has some condition of interest with two possibilities (open or closed).
- A circuit turns those two possibilities into two different **voltages**.
- The microcontroller measures the voltage and reports one of **two values** to the program.
- The program treats them as `True` or `False`, or as 1 or 0.

```text
Physical state  ──►  Voltage on a wire  ──►  Digital value  ──►  Value in the program
(door closed)         (about 0 V)             LOW                   False
(door open)           (about 3.3 V)           HIGH                  True
```

The physical state and its digital representation are *not the same thing*. The door is a door. The voltage is a voltage. The `True` in your program is a number stored in memory. The circuit designer decides how one stands for the other, and that choice can be made in more than one way. (A door sensor could be wired so that "open" is LOW instead of HIGH, and you will see why that matters shortly.)

### Binary Thinking

Many decisions in IoT systems are yes-or-no questions:

- Is the door open?
- Was motion detected?
- Is the water tank full?
- Has the button been pressed?
- Should the pump be on?

A system of this kind, with two possible states, is called **binary**. Getting used to *binary thinking* means learning to look at a physical situation and ask, "Which yes-or-no questions do I need answered, and what will each state make the system do?"

Notice that some quantities do not fit this pattern naturally. The temperature of a room is not "hot or cold". It can be any value in a range. Later readings deal with such continuous quantities. But even then, a decision often ends up being binary. "Is it too hot?" is a yes-or-no question about a continuous quantity, and the answer decides whether a fan turns on or off. Digital thinking is everywhere.

## HIGH and LOW: Two Voltage Levels

A **digital signal** carries information using just **two voltage levels**:

- **HIGH**: a voltage close to the chip's supply voltage. On the ESP32-S3 running at 3.3 V, that means roughly 3.3 V.
- **LOW**: a voltage close to 0 V, the ground level.

You met these terms in the earlier reading on GPIO. Now let us be more precise about what they mean, because a real voltage is never exactly 3.3 V or exactly 0 V. Batteries sag, wires have resistance, and electrical noise adds small fluctuations. So a digital input does not require exactly 3.3 V to call something HIGH. It has a **range** for each level, with a gap in between.

Espressif's datasheet specifies these ranges for the ESP32-S3 with a 3.3 V supply. With VDD as the supply voltage (3.3 V here):

| | Range | With VDD = 3.3 V |
|---|---|---|
| **Input read as HIGH** | at least 0.75 × VDD | above about 2.5 V |
| **Input read as LOW** | at most 0.25 × VDD | below about 0.8 V |
| **Output guaranteed HIGH** | at least 0.8 × VDD | above about 2.64 V |
| **Output guaranteed LOW** | at most 0.1 × VDD | below about 0.33 V |

```text
 3.3 V ┤ ███████  the pin can output a HIGH here (≥ about 2.64 V)
 2.5 V ┤ ███████  an input reads HIGH above about 2.5 V
       │ ░░░░░░░  the undefined zone: the input may read either way
 0.8 V ┤ ███████  an input reads LOW below about 0.8 V
 0.33 V┤ ███████  the pin can output a LOW here (≤ about 0.33 V)
   0 V ┤
```

Look at how the numbers fit together. When the chip outputs a HIGH, it produces at least about 2.64 V, which is comfortably above the 2.5 V that an input needs to read HIGH. When it outputs a LOW, it produces at most about 0.33 V, comfortably below the 0.8 V where an input reads LOW. That margin is deliberate. It means small amounts of noise do not flip a signal from one state to the other.

The zone in between is **undefined**. A voltage of 1.6 V, for example, could be read as either HIGH or LOW, and may change unpredictably. Good digital circuits avoid living there.

> **Teaching model.** For most of this course, "HIGH is about 3.3 V and LOW is about 0 V" is all you need. The ranges above explain *why* it works, and they become important when a signal looks wrong on a multimeter or behaves inconsistently.

### HIGH Does Not Mean "On"

A very common beginner misunderstanding is to think that HIGH means "on" and LOW means "off". This is *often* true, but it is not a rule of nature. **HIGH and LOW describe voltage levels. What they mean is decided by how the circuit is wired.**

- An LED wired between the pin and ground lights when the pin is HIGH.
- An LED wired between the 3.3 V supply and the pin lights when the pin is LOW, because current now flows *into* the pin.
- A button wired to ground with a pull-up reads LOW when pressed.

You already met that last case, called **active-low** behaviour: the "active" (pressed) state is LOW. The physical state, the voltage and the program's value are three different things, connected by the circuit. Keeping that in mind will save you from many confusing moments.

## Digital Signals in IoT Systems

Digital signals appear throughout IoT hardware. Here are a few places you will meet them:

| Signal | Physical state | Direction (from the microcontroller) |
|---|---|---|
| Push button | Pressed / not pressed | Into the microcontroller (input) |
| Door or window sensor | Open / closed | Input |
| Many motion-detector modules | Motion detected / not detected | Input (check the module's documentation) |
| Indicator LED | Lit / dark | Out of the microcontroller (output) |
| Buzzer module | Sounding / silent | Output |
| A transistor or relay control line | Load on / off | Output |

There is one more use worth previewing. Chips also talk to each other by sending rapid sequences of HIGH and LOW levels, which is how many sensors, displays and networks communicate. That uses exactly the same idea, a wire switching between two voltage levels, but at high speed and with agreed patterns. Later modules build on this. For now, notice how much can be done with a single wire and two states.

# Part 2 — Digital Output: Code Controls the Physical World

## The Output Direction

Recall that every GPIO pin has a **direction**, which your program chooses. When the pin is an **output**, the program is in charge, and the signal flows from the microcontroller to the component:

```text
Microcontroller  ──── signal ────►  Component (LED, transistor, buzzer module…)
   (program decides)
```

Remember that "input" and "output" are always defined from the **microcontroller's point of view**. An LED is an *output device* because the microcontroller sends a signal *out* to it.

When your program sets the value of an output pin, the chip does something simple and physical inside:

- **Setting the pin HIGH** connects it, inside the chip, to the supply voltage. The pin now sits at about 3.3 V, and can push a small current out.
- **Setting the pin LOW** connects it, inside the chip, to ground. The pin now sits at about 0 V, and can let a small current flow in.

So an output pin is like a tiny switch, controlled by your program, that connects a wire either to the supply or to ground. That is the whole trick. All the "intelligence" is in the *program* deciding when to flip it.

### What a Pin Can and Cannot Do

An output pin is a signal source, not a power supply. Everything you learned about power and current applies. An indicator LED with a series resistor draws a few milliamperes, a light load for a pin. A motor, a pump or a strip of many LEDs draws far more, and must be switched through a transistor or a driver module, as you saw earlier.

> **Assumption.** Espressif's datasheet lists current figures for the chip's pins, but they apply under specific test conditions and settings, and are not targets to aim for. In this course, keep loads on GPIO pins small, use indicator-level currents for LEDs and always use a series resistor. If you are unsure whether a component can be connected directly to a pin, check its datasheet and your board's documentation first.

## Controlling an LED from Code

You already wired an LED with a resistor to a GPIO pin, and ran a blink program. Let us go through the wiring and the code again, and this time follow each step from the line of code to the light.

```text
    GPIO pin ──[ Resistor ]──►|── GND pin
                              LED
```

Here is a shorter program than before, that turns the LED **on** and leaves it there:

```python
import board
import digitalio

led = digitalio.DigitalInOut(board.CHOSEN_LED_PIN)   # replace CHOSEN_LED_PIN
led.direction = digitalio.Direction.OUTPUT

led.value = True
```

Every GPIO program in CircuitPython follows the same short pattern:

```text
1. Choose the pin                    board.CHOSEN_LED_PIN
2. Create an object for the pin      digitalio.DigitalInOut(...)
3. Set its direction                 .direction = Direction.OUTPUT
4. Use it                            .value = True   or   .value = False
```

We can call this the **basic GPIO workflow**: *choose, create, configure, use*. You will follow it for every digital input and output you write.

Here is how each line connects to the physical world:

- `board.CHOSEN_LED_PIN` is the name CircuitPython gives to a specific pin on *your* board.
- `digitalio.DigitalInOut(...)` gives the program control of that pin. (The name means "digital input/output", a pin that can be either.)
- `led.direction = digitalio.Direction.OUTPUT` tells the chip: *this pin will send signals out.*
- `led.value = True` sets the pin HIGH. About 3.3 V appears on the pin, current flows through the resistor and LED, and the LED lights.

### Turning It On and Off, by Hand

The REPL is the best place to feel how direct this connection is. Enter the REPL and try:

```text
>>> import board
>>> import digitalio
>>> led = digitalio.DigitalInOut(board.CHOSEN_LED_PIN)
>>> led.direction = digitalio.Direction.OUTPUT
>>> led.value = True
>>> led.value = False
>>> led.value = not led.value
>>> led.value = not led.value
```

The last two lines flip the current value. `not led.value` means "the opposite of what it is now". Type each line one at a time and watch the LED. Every time you press Enter on one of these lines, the chip's pin changes voltage *immediately*.

## Program-Controlled Physical Action

When a program controls an output, it can do far more than switch it on. It decides **when** and **for how long**. That is what makes the action *program-controlled*. Here are three small programs. Read each before running it, and predict what the LED will do.

**A pattern that repeats forever:**

```python
import time
import board
import digitalio

led = digitalio.DigitalInOut(board.CHOSEN_LED_PIN)
led.direction = digitalio.Direction.OUTPUT

while True:
    led.value = True
    time.sleep(1)
    led.value = False
    time.sleep(0.25)
```

**A pattern that runs a fixed number of times, then stops:**

```python
import time
import board
import digitalio

led = digitalio.DigitalInOut(board.CHOSEN_LED_PIN)
led.direction = digitalio.Direction.OUTPUT

for i in range(3):
    led.value = True
    time.sleep(0.2)
    led.value = False
    time.sleep(0.2)

print("Done")
```

**A blink written using the "opposite" idea:**

```python
import time
import board
import digitalio

led = digitalio.DigitalInOut(board.CHOSEN_LED_PIN)
led.direction = digitalio.Direction.OUTPUT

while True:
    led.value = not led.value
    time.sleep(0.5)
```

The second program uses a `for` loop, so the pattern happens only three times, and then the program ends. Watch what the LED does after the program finishes. When a CircuitPython program ends, the board releases the pins it was using, so you should not rely on an output keeping its last state. If you need a pin to stay in a particular state, keep the program running.

> **Try it: Modify and predict.** Change one thing at a time, predict the result, then test.
> - Change the pattern in the first program to on for 0.1 seconds and off for 2 seconds. How does it look?
> - Make the LED blink three times quickly, pause for a second, and repeat.
> - In the third program, remove `time.sleep(0.5)`. What do you expect to see? What do you think is happening inside the chip?
> - Use two `time.sleep` values in the loop to create a "heartbeat" pattern (two short pulses, then a pause).

### One Program, Different Actuators

Here is something worth noticing. The program does not know what is connected to the pin. It sets a voltage. What the voltage *does* depends on what you connect:

- an LED with a resistor lights up,
- a small active buzzer module designed for microcontroller pins can sound (check its documentation for voltage and current),
- the control input of a transistor circuit can switch a motor or pump on and off.

The code is essentially identical: `value = True` or `value = False`. That is the beauty of digital output. A single yes-or-no signal is enough to turn many kinds of actuators on and off, as long as the circuit between the pin and the actuator is right.

### An Output That Is "Backwards"

Earlier we saw that HIGH does not always mean "on". Here is a concrete case. Suppose an LED is wired between the **3.3 V pin** and the GPIO pin, through a resistor:

```text
    3.3 V ──[ Resistor ]──►|── GPIO pin
                           LED
```

When the pin is LOW, current flows from 3.3 V through the resistor and LED *into* the pin, so the LED lights. When the pin is HIGH, both ends of the LED circuit are at nearly the same voltage, so no current flows and the LED is dark.

> **Try it: Predict before testing.** For this wiring, what will `led.value = True` do? What about `led.value = False`? Predict, then wire it and check. What does this tell you about the relationship between what your program says and what the LED does?

### When an Output Does Not Behave

If an output does not do what you expect, use the same order of reasoning you learned in the previous reading. You are looking for the first point at which reality departs from your prediction:

```text
Is the program running?  ─►  Does the pin change state?  ─►  Is the wiring right?  ─►  Is the component OK?
```

1. Is the program running and reaching the line that changes the pin? (Add a `print` to check.)
2. Does the pin's voltage change? Measure it, or set it by hand in the REPL.
3. Is the wiring correct, with the resistor in series, the LED the right way round and a shared ground?
4. Is the component healthy?

The multimeter tells you whether the problem is on the software side or the hardware side of the pin.

# Part 3 — Digital Input: The Physical World Talks to Code

## The Input Direction

So far, the signal has flowed *out* of the microcontroller. Now we reverse it. When a pin is configured as an **input**, the physical world is in charge. The signal flows *into* the microcontroller, and the program **reads** it:

```text
Component (button, switch, digital sensor…)  ──── signal ────►  Microcontroller
                                                                 (program reads it)
```

An input pin does not drive anything. It **measures** the voltage on the wire connected to it and reports whether that voltage counts as HIGH or LOW, using the ranges you saw earlier. In effect, it is a very sensitive digital voltmeter. Espressif's datasheet gives the input current of a pin as at most 50 nanoamperes, which is almost nothing. This has two consequences:

- An input pin **cannot power a component**. Connecting an LED to an input pin does nothing, because the pin supplies no current. Inputs *listen*, and outputs *drive*.
- An input pin is easily influenced by whatever is connected to it, or by whatever *is nearby* if nothing is connected. This second point is the key to reading a button correctly.

## Reading a Button

Recall from the earlier reading on components that a button does not produce a voltage by itself. It only opens or closes a path. If you connect one side of a button to ground and the other to an input pin, the pin reads LOW when the button is pressed, but when the button is released, **the pin is connected to nothing**. It is **floating**, with no defined voltage, and it may read HIGH or LOW at random as it picks up electrical noise.

The solution was a **pull-up resistor**: a resistor from the pin to the supply voltage, so that the pin has a default HIGH when nothing else is driving it. A **pull-down resistor** does the opposite, pulling the pin to LOW by default.

### Using the Chip's Built-In Pull Resistors

You do not need to add a separate resistor. GPIO pins on the ESP32-S3 have **internal weak pull-up and pull-down resistors** that the program can switch on. Espressif's datasheet gives their typical value as about 45 kΩ. In CircuitPython, this is a single line:

```python
button.pull = digitalio.Pull.UP
```

This makes the button circuit very simple. Connect one side of the button to the input pin and the other to a **GND** pin, and turn on the internal pull-up:

```text
    3.3 V
      │
   [ ~45 kΩ ]        ← inside the chip; enabled by Pull.UP
      │
      ├────────► input pin
      │
   [ Button ]        ← the only part you wire
      │
     GND
```

Now the behaviour is predictable:

| Button | Pin voltage | Digital state | `button.value` |
|---|---|---|---|
| Released | About 3.3 V (pulled up) | HIGH | `True` |
| Pressed | About 0 V (connected to GND) | LOW | `False` |

This is the **active-low** behaviour: pressing the button makes the pin go LOW. Notice what looks backwards. A pressed button gives `False`.

Let us use Ohm's Law to check that this is sensible. When the button is pressed, the ~45 kΩ pull-up sits between 3.3 V and ground, so the current is about 3.3 V ÷ 45,000 Ω ≈ 73 µA (microamperes), which is tiny. The pull-up is called "weak" for a reason. It is strong enough to hold the pin HIGH when nothing else is connected, but it wastes almost no energy when the button pulls the pin to ground.

### The Other Way: Pull-Down

You can also wire a button the other way round. Connect one side of the button to **3.3 V** and the other to the input pin, and turn on the internal pull-**down**:

```python
button.pull = digitalio.Pull.DOWN
```

Now the released button gives LOW (`False`) and the pressed button gives HIGH (`True`), which feels more natural. Both work. Which one you choose depends on the circuit, and on what the board and other parts already do. The important thing is to know which one *you* have built, because it decides how you interpret the value.

> **Assumption.** This reading uses a button wired to ground with the internal pull-up, the simplest and most common arrangement. Some pins on some boards may already be connected to on-board resistors or buttons, so check that your chosen pin has no special role. On the ESP32-S3, for instance, Espressif identifies certain pins (GPIO0, GPIO3, GPIO45 and GPIO46) as **strapping pins** that are read at start-up to configure the chip, and the native USB uses GPIO19 and GPIO20. Avoid these for your first experiments, and choose a plain general-purpose pin from your board's pinout diagram.

> **Try it: Predict the pin voltage.** Before wiring, fill in this table on paper for the pull-up arrangement. Then build it with a multimeter between the pin and GND, and check.
>
> | State | Predicted voltage | Predicted `button.value` |
> |---|---|---|
> | Button released | | |
> | Button pressed | | |
>
> Then repeat for the pull-down arrangement. Were you surprised by anything?

### Seeing the Floating Problem for Yourself

You can observe the reason for pull resistors directly. Wire a button between the input pin and ground, but this time do **not** enable a pull resistor:

```python
button.pull = None
```

Print the value repeatedly while the button is released, and while you bring a finger near the pin or wire. Some readings may flicker between `True` and `False` with no button press at all. This is a floating input, and it is exactly what the pull-up prevents.

## Reading the State in Code

Now we write the program. Here is the input version of the GPIO workflow: *choose, create, configure, use*. There is one extra step, which is the pull resistor.

```python
import time
import board
import digitalio

button = digitalio.DigitalInOut(board.CHOSEN_BUTTON_PIN)   # replace CHOSEN_BUTTON_PIN
button.direction = digitalio.Direction.INPUT
button.pull = digitalio.Pull.UP

while True:
    print(button.value)
    time.sleep(0.1)
```

Here is how it works:

- `digitalio.DigitalInOut(board.CHOSEN_BUTTON_PIN)` claims the pin, as before.
- `button.direction = digitalio.Direction.INPUT` says the pin will *listen*. (An input is also the default state when you create a `DigitalInOut`, but writing it out makes the program clearer.)
- `button.pull = digitalio.Pull.UP` switches on the internal pull-up resistor.
- `button.value` **reads** the pin at that moment. It gives `True` if the pin is HIGH and `False` if it is LOW.
- `print(...)` shows the value in the serial console, since the board has no screen. `time.sleep(0.1)` makes the program check ten times per second.

Run the program and watch the console. You should see a stream of `True` while the button is released, and `False` while you hold it down.

A program that keeps checking an input over and over is said to be **polling** it. Polling is the simplest way to watch an input: ask, wait a moment, ask again.

> **Try it: Observe and explain.** With the program running:
> 1. What do you see when the button is released? Pressed? Does it match your prediction?
> 2. Press the button for one second. Roughly how many `False` values do you expect? Count them. Why that number?
> 3. What happens if you change `time.sleep(0.1)` to `time.sleep(2)` and tap the button quickly? What does that tell you about how often the program looks at the input?

### Translating Electrical States into Meaning

The value `False` for a *pressed* button is confusing to read. A common habit is to give the value a name that says what it *means*:

```python
pressed = not button.value
```

Now `pressed` is `True` when the button is pressed and `False` when it is not. The code carries the same information but reads like a sentence. This is the "digital representation of a physical state" idea from the start of this reading, turned into code. The pin has a voltage, the voltage becomes `button.value`, and your program turns that into `pressed`, a name for something that matters in the physical world.

## Making a Decision: The Button Controls the LED

We now have all the pieces. We can read an input, and we can control an output. Connecting them means the program makes a **decision**: *if the button is pressed, then the LED is on.*

### Building the Circuit

You now have two separate circuits connected to two separate pins, sharing the board's ground:

```text
     Button circuit                       LED circuit

   input pin ──┬── [ Button ] ── GND      output pin ──[ Resistor ]──►|── GND
               │                                                      LED
        (internal pull-up
        switched on in code)
```

Build it in this order, as always keeping to the safety habits from earlier:

1. **Disconnect USB** so the board is unpowered while you wire.
2. **Wire the LED circuit**: output pin → resistor → LED anode, LED cathode → GND.
3. **Wire the button**: one side to the input pin, the other side to GND.
4. **Check** each connection against the diagram. Are the two pins different? Is the LED the right way round?
5. **Reconnect USB.**

### The Program

```python
import time
import board
import digitalio

# Replace both placeholders with pin names from your own dir(board)
led = digitalio.DigitalInOut(board.CHOSEN_LED_PIN)
led.direction = digitalio.Direction.OUTPUT

button = digitalio.DigitalInOut(board.CHOSEN_BUTTON_PIN)
button.direction = digitalio.Direction.INPUT
button.pull = digitalio.Pull.UP

while True:
    pressed = not button.value      # True while the button is held down

    if pressed:
        led.value = True            # button held: LED on
    else:
        led.value = False           # button released: LED off

    time.sleep(0.01)
```

Read it as a story. Each time round the loop, the program **senses** the button (`button.value`), **decides** (`if pressed`) and **acts** (`led.value = ...`). Then it waits a hundredth of a second and repeats, hundreds of times a second.

That short pause gives the processor a breather, and that is all it does here. It is **not** a solution to the reliability problems that mechanical buttons cause. We come back to those shortly, and to their proper solutions in the next reading.

There is a shorter way to write the same logic, since the LED should simply copy the state of the button:

```python
led.value = pressed
```

The longer `if`/`else` form is easier to *read* and, more importantly, easier to *change*. When you want the LED to do something more interesting than copy the button, the `if` is where you add it.

### Following One Press from Start to Finish

Let us follow a single button press all the way through the system, one step at a time:

```text
1. You press the button.                           PHYSICAL INPUT
2. The button closes; the pin is connected to GND.
3. The pin voltage falls to about 0 V (LOW).        DIGITAL SIGNAL
4. The chip reads the pin: button.value is False.   MICROCONTROLLER
5. The program sets pressed = True.                 PROGRAM LOGIC
6. The if-branch runs: led.value = True.
7. The output pin is driven HIGH (about 3.3 V).     DIGITAL OUTPUT
8. Current flows through the resistor and LED.
9. The LED lights.                                  PHYSICAL ACTION
```

This is the full chain of the system, and it is worth reading twice:

```text
Physical Input → Digital Signal → Microcontroller → Program Logic → Digital Output → Physical Action
```

Every IoT system you build in this course, however complex it becomes, follows this same shape.

> **Try it: Predict, measure, explain.** Before you press anything:

> 2. **Measure.** Check each with a multimeter, for both button states.
> 3. **Explain.** If any reading differs from your prediction, what does the difference tell you?

### Changing the Logic

Because the behaviour lives in the program, you can change it without touching the wiring. This is one of the great advantages of a microcontroller. Try these, one at a time, and predict the result each time:

- **Invert it.** Make the LED light when the button is *released*, and go dark when it is pressed. What one word changes?
- **Add a message.** Print `"Pressed"` or `"Released"` while the LED changes.
- **Add a second behaviour.** Make the LED stay off when the button is pressed but blink when it is released.
- **Predict before testing.** What happens if you delete the line `button.pull = digitalio.Pull.UP`? Predict what the LED does, then check. Does the effect depend on where your hand is?

## Reacting to Changes

So far, the LED simply copies the button: on while it is held, off when it is released. That is a decision based on the *current state*. But often what matters is not that the button *is* pressed, but that it **just became** pressed. A doorbell should ring once when you press, not repeatedly while you hold.

Detecting a **change** in an input is a different question from reading its state. We call the moment of change an **event**: for example "the button was just pressed". To spot an event, the program needs a memory of what the input looked like *last time*, and compares it to what it looks like *now*.

```text
time →           0    1    2    3    4    5    6    7
pressed now:     no   no   YES  YES  YES  no   no   no
pressed before:  no   no   no   YES  YES  YES  no   no

just pressed?    -    -    EVENT -    -    -    -    -
just released?   -    -    -    -    -    EVENT -    -
```

A "just pressed" event happens when the button is pressed now *and was not* before. A "just released" event is the opposite. Here is a program that toggles the LED every time the button is pressed:

```python
import time
import board
import digitalio

led = digitalio.DigitalInOut(board.CHOSEN_LED_PIN)
led.direction = digitalio.Direction.OUTPUT

button = digitalio.DigitalInOut(board.CHOSEN_BUTTON_PIN)
button.direction = digitalio.Direction.INPUT
button.pull = digitalio.Pull.UP

was_pressed = False          # what the button looked like last time round

while True:
    pressed = not button.value

    if pressed and not was_pressed:      # the moment of pressing
        led.value = not led.value        # flip the LED

    was_pressed = pressed                # remember for next time
    time.sleep(0.01)
```

The important line is `if pressed and not was_pressed`. The `was_pressed` variable is the program's **memory** of the previous reading, stored in the board's working memory (its RAM). Without it, holding the button would flip the LED hundreds of times a second.

> **Try it: Predict, then test.**
> - Press and hold the button for several seconds. Does the LED flip once or many times? Why?
> - Change the program to toggle on *release* instead of on press. Which line changes?
> - Add a counter that prints the total number of presses. Where does the counter go?

> **A note on what you might notice.** If you press the button many times, you may occasionally see the LED flip twice for one press, or a press seem to be missed. That is not a mistake in your program. Mechanical buttons **bounce**: as the metal contacts meet, they briefly connect and disconnect several times in a few milliseconds, and a fast program can read each of those as a separate press. You met this idea, called contact bounce, in the earlier reading on components. Making button inputs reliable, including the techniques for handling bounce, is the subject of the next reading. For now, when you see this behaviour, you will know what causes it.

## Digital Sensing in the Loop

A button is the simplest digital sensor, and it fits the model we have used from the start:

```text
Sense:    the button detects a finger (a physical state)
Compute:  the program reads the pin, interprets it and decides
Actuate:  the program sets the LED pin
```

Now notice that *nothing in the program is specific to a button*. Any component that produces a HIGH or LOW signal can be connected to an input pin and read in exactly the same way:

- A **slide switch or toggle switch** behaves like a button that stays in position.
- A **door or window sensor** is a switch that opens or closes when a door moves.
- Some **motion-detector modules** produce a digital HIGH/LOW output (check the module's documentation).

Suppose you connect a door switch instead of a button, and change the meaning of the words:

```python
door_open = not sensor.value        # meaning depends on how the switch is wired

if door_open:
    led.value = True                # warning light on
else:
    led.value = False
```

The code has the same structure. Only the *names* changed, and the assumption about which state means "open". That assumption depends entirely on how the sensor is wired, which is why you should always test a new sensor by printing its value in each physical state before writing the rest of your program.

> **Assumption.** When you use a module (such as a motion sensor) instead of a bare button, check its operating voltage, what its output signal levels are and how many pins it has. A module powered from 5 V may produce a 5 V output, which must not be connected directly to a 3.3 V input pin without checking. Always read the module's documentation.

# Putting It All Together

## The Complete Flow

You now understand every link in the chain that connects the physical world to a program and back:

| Stage | What happens | Where it lives |
|---|---|---|
| **Physical input** | A finger presses a button | The physical world |
| **Digital signal** | The pin goes LOW (about 0 V) | The wire, and the pin's voltage |
| **Microcontroller** | The pin is read as `False` | The GPIO input |
| **Program logic** | `pressed = not button.value`, then `if pressed:` | Your code |
| **Digital output** | `led.value = True` drives the pin HIGH | The GPIO output |
| **Physical action** | Current flows and the LED lights | The physical world |

Read it as the loop you learned at the start of the course:

```text
Sense (button) → Compute (your program) → Actuate (LED)
```

You began this reading with the LED recipe from your first programs. You can now explain it, and extend it.
