# Part 1 — Actuation: Turning Decisions into Physical Actions

## Sensing and Actuation

### The Two Halves of the Loop

Every system in this course has two kinds of connection to the physical world, and it helps to keep them firmly apart:

| | Sensing | Actuation |
|---|---|---|
| **Direction** | Physical world → microcontroller | Microcontroller → physical world |
| **Parts** | Sensors, buttons, switches | LEDs, buzzers, motors, relays |
| **What flows** | Information | Energy, controlled by information |
| **GPIO role** | Input | Output |
| **Typical power** | Small | Often larger |

Sensing *gathers* information about the world. Actuation *changes* the world. A sensor is the system's senses, and an actuator is its hands.

### What an Actuator Does

An **actuator** converts an electrical signal, and the electrical energy behind it, into a physical effect: light, sound, motion, heat or a flow. The important word is **energy**. A sensor only needs to whisper its information. An actuator has to do *work*. Light must be emitted, air must be pushed to make sound, a motor must turn.

That is why actuators need care about power, as you saw earlier. A GPIO pin carries the *command*, and something has to supply the *energy*.

### How a Program's Decision Becomes a Physical Action

You already know the chain for an LED, and it is the same chain for every actuator:

```text
Program decision           led.value = True
      │
      ▼
Digital value              True
      │
      ▼
GPIO output pin            driven HIGH (about 3.3 V)
      │
      ▼
Electrical current         flows through a resistor and the actuator
      │
      ▼
Physical effect            light, sound, motion…
```

Each arrow is a translation from one form to another: an instruction in a program becomes a number, a number becomes a voltage on a pin, a voltage becomes a current and a current becomes something a person can see, hear or feel. Being able to say *what form the signal takes at each step* is exactly the skill of tracing a signal path.

## Digital Control of Actuators

The simplest way to control an actuator is **digitally**: a two-state signal turns it fully on or fully off. You have done this with an LED. The same idea works for many actuators:

| Actuator | Digital control means | How it is usually connected |
|---|---|---|
| **LED** | On or off | GPIO pin → resistor → LED → ground |
| **Active buzzer** | Sounding or silent | Directly from a pin (if its documentation says so), or through a transistor |
| **Relay module** | Switch a load on or off | A control input on a relay module, or a transistor circuit |
| **Motor or pump** | Running or stopped | Through a transistor or motor-driver module, with protection |

The code is the same each time: `value = True` or `value = False`. What differs is the circuit between the pin and the device. A pin can supply only a small current. For a small, low-power indicator, connecting directly (with a resistor for an LED) is fine. For anything that needs more current, a transistor or a driver module sits between the pin and the load, exactly as in the earlier reading on components.

> **Assumption.** Always check the documentation of the actuator or module you use. Ask three questions before connecting anything to a pin: What voltage does it need? How much current does it draw? Does it have a built-in driver, or does it need one?

## The Buzzer

A **buzzer** turns an electrical signal into sound, which makes it a good complement to an LED. An LED is silent and easy to miss, and a buzzer can get attention from across a room. Many IoT devices, such as alarms, timers and appliances, use one.

### Active and Passive Buzzers

There are two kinds, and they look alike. Recall the distinction from earlier:

- An **active buzzer** contains its own tone generator. Apply a steady voltage and it makes a fixed tone. Digital control is exactly what it needs: HIGH sounds it, LOW silences it.
- A **passive buzzer** has no tone generator. It needs a rapidly changing signal from the microcontroller, and the *frequency* of that signal sets the pitch. That needs a technique introduced later in the course, so this reading uses an **active buzzer**.

> **Note.** If a buzzer stays silent when you set its pin HIGH, or sounds only as faint clicks, ask which type you have. A passive buzzer driven with a steady voltage does not produce a continuous tone.

### Connecting a Buzzer

Buzzers come in two forms:

- **A buzzer module**, which has a small board with power, ground and signal pins, and often includes a transistor driver. Its signal pin is designed to be driven from a microcontroller. Check the module's documentation for its supply voltage and whether its signal input accepts 3.3 V.
- **A bare buzzer**, with just two leads. It may draw more current than a GPIO pin should supply, so it is normally switched through a transistor, as you saw earlier.

Active buzzers have a **polarity**, usually marked with a plus sign. Connect the positive side to the supply (or driver output) and the other side to ground.

```text
   Buzzer module                        Board

   VCC  ─────────────────────────────  3.3 V or 5 V (as the documentation says)
   GND  ─────────────────────────────  GND
   SIG  ─────────────────────────────  output pin
```

### Controlling a Buzzer from Code

Here is a program that sounds a buzzer in short beeps:

```python
import time
import board
import digitalio

buzzer = digitalio.DigitalInOut(board.CHOSEN_BUZZER_PIN)   # replace with your pin
buzzer.direction = digitalio.Direction.OUTPUT

while True:
    buzzer.value = True          # sound on
    time.sleep(0.2)
    buzzer.value = False         # sound off
    time.sleep(0.8)
```

It follows the same *choose, create, configure, use* pattern you know. Only the name changed, from `led` to `buzzer`. The program does not know or care what is connected. That is one of the strengths of digital output, and it means everything you learned about LED patterns transfers directly.

> **Try it: Predict, then test.**
> 1. Predict how the buzzer will sound with the program above. Then run it.
> 2. Change the pattern so that it beeps three times quickly, then pauses for two seconds, and repeats.
> 3. Some buzzers are active-low (they sound when the pin is LOW). If yours sounds when you expected silence, what does that tell you about the module, and what one change in the code would compensate?
> 4. **A sound-level caution.** Do not hold a buzzer against your ear, and keep the beeps short while you are experimenting.

## LED-Based Output

A single LED is a very small output. You can say more with LEDs than "on" and "off":

- **Patterns.** A slow blink and a fast blink can mean different things. The same LED can signal "normal", "warning" and "error" by pattern alone.
- **Several LEDs.** Multiple LEDs, each on its own pin with its own resistor, can show a *level*, like the bars on a signal-strength indicator. As you will see later in this reading, this is a natural way to display an analog value.
- **Colour.** LEDs of different colours communicate meaning quickly. Green for OK, red for a problem.

When you use several LEDs, give each one its own **series resistor** and its own **pin**. Never share a single resistor between two LEDs on different pins, and never connect an LED to a pin without a resistor.

Here is a program that keeps three LEDs in a list and turns them on one at a time, in order:

```python
import time
import board
import digitalio

led_pins = [board.CHOSEN_LED_PIN_1, board.CHOSEN_LED_PIN_2, board.CHOSEN_LED_PIN_3]

leds = []
for pin in led_pins:
    led = digitalio.DigitalInOut(pin)
    led.direction = digitalio.Direction.OUTPUT
    leds.append(led)

while True:
    for led in leds:
        led.value = True
        time.sleep(0.3)
        led.value = False
```

The list and the `for` loops mean you can add a fourth LED by adding one pin to the list, with no other changes.

## Program Logic Controlling Physical Actions

An actuator is only useful when something decides when to use it. That decision is the "Compute" in Sense → Compute → Actuate. A useful way to plan it is a **decision table**: a list of situations, and what the outputs should do in each.

For example, a simple status indicator for a device:

| Situation | LED | Buzzer |
|---|---|---|
| Everything normal | Slow blink | Silent |
| Warning | Fast blink | Silent |
| Fault | Steady on | Short beeps |

Writing the table first, before any code, makes the program easy to write and easy to check. Each row becomes a branch in an `if` / `elif` / `else` structure, and each cell becomes an assignment to an output. Later in this reading, the "situation" will come from an analog input.

> **Try it: Plan, then build.** Write a decision table for a device with three states: idle, active and error. Choose an LED pattern and a buzzer behaviour for each state. Then write a program that steps through the three states, spending a few seconds in each, and check that the outputs match your table. Where does your program keep track of *which state it is in*?

# Part 2 — The Analog World

## Continuous Quantities

### The Physical World Varies Smoothly

Think about how many of the quantities around you can be described as "on or off". A light switch, yes. But now think about the *brightness* of a room through the day. It rises slowly through the morning, stays high, and falls gradually into evening. There is no moment at which it jumps from "dark" to "bright".

Most physical quantities behave this way:

- **Temperature** rises and falls smoothly. It can be 24.0 °C, 24.1 °C, 24.13 °C.
- **Light** can be any level between darkness and full sun.
- **Sound** is a pressure that varies continuously.
- **Distance** can be any length.
- **The position of a knob** can be anywhere from fully left to fully right.

We call such quantities **continuous**: between any two values there are always more values. A quantity like this, when represented by a voltage, gives us an **analog signal**.

### Digital and Analog Signals

You know digital signals well by now. Here they are side by side:

```text
Digital signal (two states):         Analog signal (any value in a range):

  3.3 V ─┐     ┌───┐                   3.3 V ┤      ╭──╮
         │     │   │                         │    ╭─╯  ╰─╮     ╭─
     0 V └─────┘   └────                 0 V ┤ ───╯        ╰───╯
         time →                               time →
```

| | Digital signal | Analog signal |
|---|---|---|
| **Possible values** | Two (HIGH or LOW) | Any value in a range |
| **Represents** | A yes-or-no condition | A quantity that varies smoothly |
| **Example** | A button pressed or not | The voltage from a knob, or from a temperature sensor |
| **How the microcontroller reads it** | A digital input, using thresholds | An ADC, which measures the voltage and reports a number |
| **Information carried** | One bit at a time | A whole range of values |

> **Teaching model.** Real digital signals are also voltages, and real voltages vary continuously. What makes a signal "digital" is that we *decide* to treat only two ranges of voltage as meaningful, ignoring the values in between. An analog signal keeps the whole range meaningful. The difference lies in how we interpret the voltage.

### Voltage as a Measurable Analog Quantity

You have been measuring voltage with a multimeter since the beginning of the course. Voltage is a quantity that can take *any* value. That makes it a natural way to represent other continuous quantities. Many sensors work by producing a **voltage that changes with a physical condition**:

```text
Physical quantity ──► Sensor ──► Voltage that varies with it
  (brightness)     (LDR divider)   (1.2 V, 1.9 V, 2.6 V…)
```

Recall the LDR and fixed resistor from the first module: a voltage divider whose output goes up as the light increases. That output voltage is an analog signal. It carries *how much* light there is, not just whether it is "light" or "dark".

So we need a way for a microcontroller, which works with numbers, to read a voltage that can be anything. That is the job of the ADC.

### The Gap Between the Two Worlds

Here is the difficulty in one sentence:

> **The physical world is often continuous, but a microcontroller processes numbers.**

A number in a program is stored using a fixed number of bits, so it can only take a fixed set of values. To bring a continuous voltage into a program, we must **measure** it and **round** the result to one of those values. That is what an ADC does.

# Part 3 — Reading Analog Values

## The ADC

### What an ADC Does

An **analog-to-digital converter (ADC)** is a circuit that measures a voltage and expresses it as a whole number. It is a peripheral of the microcontroller, one of the blocks from the map you met earlier, alongside GPIO. The ESP32-S3 has ADCs built in.

A useful picture is a **ruler**. Imagine a voltage as a length, and the ADC as a ruler with a fixed number of tick marks. Measuring means finding which tick mark the voltage is nearest to, and reporting the *number* of that mark. You can never report a value between marks: you *round* to the nearest one.

```text
Voltage:    0 V                                                   3.3 V
            │─────┬─────┬─────┬─────┬─────┬─────┬─────┬─────┬─────│
Count:      0     1     2     3     4     5     6     7     8   ...
                                 ▲
                      an input of about this voltage is reported as "3"
```

The result, a whole number, is what your program receives. From then on, it is just a number, and you can compare it, add to it, scale it and use it in decisions.

```text
Voltage ──► [ ADC ] ──► Number ──► Program logic
```

### Sampling, Intuitively

An ADC does not watch a voltage continuously. Each time you ask for a reading, it takes a **sample**: a quick measurement of the voltage *at that moment*, and reports it. Reading in a loop produces a series of samples over time.

Think of a video. It is a series of still photographs, taken many times a second. If it takes enough photographs per second, the motion looks smooth. Similarly, if you sample often enough compared with how fast the voltage changes, your series of samples describes the signal faithfully. If the voltage changes faster than you sample, you miss what happened in between.

For a knob that a person turns by hand, a few readings per second are plenty. For fast signals, such as sound, sampling has to be much faster. That is a matter for later.

### Resolution

How many tick marks does the ruler have? That is the **resolution** of the ADC. It is described in **bits**. An ADC with *n* bits has 2ⁿ possible output values, and divides its input range into that many levels.

If we imagine a 3.3 V range:

| Resolution | Number of levels | Size of one step (over 3.3 V) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 bit | 2 | 1.65 V (this is really a digital input) |
| 3 bits | 8 | about 0.41 V |
| 8 bits | 256 | about 12.9 mV |
| 10 bits | 1,024 | about 3.2 mV |
| 12 bits | 4,096 | about 0.81 mV |
| 16 bits | 65,536 | about 0.05 mV |

**Resolution tells you how finely the ADC can tell voltages apart.** A higher resolution means smaller steps, so you can detect smaller changes. At 3 bits, any voltage within 0.41 V of another gets rounded to the same number. At 12 bits, differences of less than a millivolt can produce different numbers.

The table has a satisfying detail: a 1-bit ADC is just a digital input, with two possible answers. Digital input is the simplest possible case of analog-to-digital conversion.

### The ESP32-S3's ADC and CircuitPython's Numbers

According to Espressif's documentation, the ESP32-S3's ADCs have **12-bit** resolution: 4,096 levels, numbered 0 to 4095. But when you read an analog input in CircuitPython, you will see numbers from 0 to **65,535**, which is a 16-bit range. Why the difference?

CircuitPython gives the same range on every board. Its documentation says the value is always between 0 and 65535, *even if the underlying ADC has a lower resolution*. It scales the hardware's result up to that common range so that programs are portable between boards.

This has a consequence worth knowing. On a 12-bit ADC, the values you see in CircuitPython change in **steps of about 16** (65,536 ÷ 4,096 = 16). You will not see every number from 0 to 65535.

```text
Hardware ADC:   0        1        2        3    …   4095        (12-bit)
                │        │        │        │            │
                ×16      ×16      ×16      ×16          ×16
                ▼        ▼        ▼        ▼            ▼
CircuitPython:  0       16       32       48    …    ≈65520       (16-bit scale)
```

> **Resolution is not the same as accuracy.** The 16-bit range in CircuitPython does *not* mean the measurement is accurate to 16 bits. The hardware measures with 12-bit resolution, and even that number is subject to noise and other errors, as you will see. A ruler with very fine marks is only as good as its manufacture.

### From Voltage to Number, and Back

For an *ideal* ADC, the relationship between the input voltage and the number is a simple proportion:

```text
fraction of the range = input voltage ÷ full-scale voltage
CircuitPython value   ≈ fraction × 65535
```

Going the other way:

```text
input voltage ≈ (value ÷ 65535) × full-scale voltage
```

> **Example values.** Suppose the full-scale voltage is 3.3 V.
> - An input of 1.65 V is half of the range, so the value is about 0.5 × 65535 ≈ **32,768**.
> - A value of 49,152 is 49,152 ÷ 65,535 = 0.75 of the range, so the voltage is about 0.75 × 3.3 V ≈ **2.48 V**.

Notice the phrase *ideal ADC*. Real ADCs depart from the ideal in several ways. This matters a great deal for the ESP32-S3, so let us be careful.

### Reality Check: The ADC Is Not Ideal

According to Espressif's documentation, the ESP32-S3's ADC has a limited range of accurately measurable input voltage. It is normally used with **input attenuation**, a circuit that scales the input down so that larger voltages fit. At the highest attenuation setting, Espressif documents a measurement range of about 0 to 3100 mV, and states that the effective measurement range is about 2900 mV. Voltages above the effective range give undefined results, and readings can saturate near the top.

CircuitPython's documentation adds a warning of its own: the ADC value may not scale to the actual voltage linearly at the ends of the range. Adafruit's ESP32-S3 board guide, in its voltage example, converts values to volts using a scaling constant that reflects the ADC's limited usable range (about 2.57 V at a reading near 51,000), rather than assuming the full 3.3 V.

The practical conclusions are:

- **Do not assume 65535 means 3.3 V.** On your board, the highest value may occur at a lower voltage, and the reading may flatten out before the very top of the range.
- **The scale may not be perfectly linear**, especially near 0 V and near the top.
- **Readings are noisy.** The same voltage may give slightly different numbers each time.
- **Never apply more than 3.3 V** to an analog input pin. Voltages above the supply can damage the chip.

> **Teaching model.** For learning, we treat the ADC as "0 to about 3.3 V maps roughly linearly onto 0 to about 65535", and we *check* that assumption by measuring. The habit of comparing a reading with a multimeter is worth more than any single number in this reading.

## The Potentiometer as an Analog Input

To practise reading analog values, we need an analog signal that we control. A **potentiometer** (often shortened to *pot*) is exactly that: a knob that produces a voltage you can set by hand.

### How a Potentiometer Works

A potentiometer is a **variable resistor** with three terminals. Inside is a strip of resistive material, with a fixed terminal at each end. A third terminal, the **wiper**, slides along the strip as you turn the knob.

```text
   Terminal 1 ──┬─────────────┐
                │             │
                │  resistive  │
                │    track    │◄──── wiper (Terminal 2, the middle pin)
                │             │        slides as the knob turns
   Terminal 3 ──┴─────────────┘
```

The total resistance from terminal 1 to terminal 3 is fixed (for example 10 kΩ). As you turn the knob, the wiper divides that track into two resistances: one between the wiper and terminal 1, and one between the wiper and terminal 3. The two always add up to the total.

If you connect terminal 1 to **3.3 V** and terminal 3 to **ground**, you have built a **voltage divider**, exactly the one you know from the first module. The wiper is the output:

```text
    3.3 V ─── Terminal 1
                  │
          ┌───────┤
          │   R_top (wiper to terminal 1)
          │       │
   Wiper ─┼───────┼───────► to the analog input pin
          │       │
          │   R_bottom (wiper to terminal 3)
          │       │
    GND ──── Terminal 3
```

```text
V_wiper = 3.3 V × R_bottom ÷ (R_top + R_bottom)
```

- With the knob at one end, the wiper is at 0 V.
- With the knob in the middle, the wiper is at about half the supply.
- With the knob at the other end, the wiper is at about 3.3 V.

So a potentiometer is a **controllable analog voltage source**. You turn the knob, and the voltage changes smoothly from 0 V to the supply voltage. Notice how it connects with what you know already: it is nothing more than the voltage divider from the start of the course, with a sliding tap.

You can even check the current using Ohm's Law. For an example 10 kΩ potentiometer across 3.3 V, the current through the whole track is 3.3 V ÷ 10,000 Ω = **0.33 mA**, tiny and steady, whatever position the knob is in.

> **Assumption.** Potentiometers come in different total resistances and in different *tapers*, which describe how resistance changes with rotation. A **linear** taper changes evenly, and is the best choice for this reading. Check the documentation or markings of yours. The example values use 10 kΩ, but any value in a similar range works.

### Wiring the Potentiometer

Build it in this order, with the board unpowered:

1. **Disconnect USB.**
2. **Connect one outer terminal to the 3.3 V pin** of the board. Use the 3.3 V pin, *not* 5 V, because the wiper's voltage goes to the analog input and must never exceed 3.3 V.
3. **Connect the other outer terminal to a GND pin.**
4. **Connect the middle terminal (the wiper)** to the analog-capable pin you chose.
5. **Check the wiring**, and then reconnect USB.

Turning the knob one way will then increase the voltage, and the other way will decrease it. If it goes the wrong way for your liking, swap the two outer connections.

### Reading It in CircuitPython

CircuitPython provides analog input through its `analogio` module. The pattern is the same as for digital pins:

```python
import time
import board
import analogio

pot = analogio.AnalogIn(board.CHOSEN_ANALOG_PIN)     # replace with your analog-capable pin

while True:
    print(pot.value)
    time.sleep(0.2)
```

What this does:

- `import analogio` brings in the module for analog inputs.
- `analogio.AnalogIn(pin)` sets up the pin as an analog input, connected to the ADC. There is no direction to set, since an analog input is always an input.
- `pot.value` **reads** the ADC and returns a number from 0 to 65535.
- `print(...)` shows the value in the serial console, and `time.sleep(0.2)` takes five samples per second.

Open the serial console and turn the knob. You should see the number rise and fall smoothly, from near one end of the range to near the other.

> **Try it: Predict, observe, explain.**
> 1. **Predict.** Before running the program, predict what you will see with the knob fully one way, in the middle and fully the other way.
> 2. **Observe.** Run it. What values do you actually see at the three positions? Do they reach 0 and 65535?
> 3. **Notice the steps.** Turn the knob very slowly. Do the numbers change by one at a time, or in jumps? What does that tell you about the resolution?
> 4. **Explain.** If the top value falls short of 65535, what might explain it, given what you read about the ADC?
> 5. **Compare with digital.** In the previous reading, a button gave two possible values. How does this differ in the amount of information each reading carries?

### From Raw Numbers to Voltages

The raw numbers do not say much on their own. A first step is to convert to voltage:

```python
import time
import board
import analogio

pot = analogio.AnalogIn(board.CHOSEN_ANALOG_PIN)

def to_volts(raw, full_scale=3.3):
    return raw / 65535 * full_scale

while True:
    raw = pot.value
    print(raw, "->", round(to_volts(raw), 2), "V")
    time.sleep(0.2)
```

The function uses the ideal formula from earlier, with an assumed 3.3 V full scale. Now you can *check* it.

> **Try it: Compare with a multimeter.** Measure the voltage at the potentiometer's wiper with a multimeter while the program runs, at several knob positions. Record the multimeter voltage and the program's calculated voltage in a table.
> - Do they agree at the middle of the range?
> - Do they disagree near the ends?
> - What does the disagreement tell you about assuming the ideal formula?

### Raw Readings and Real Quantities

An ADC reading is a **raw value**: a number that reports how the ADC measured a voltage. It is *not automatically* a measurement of what the voltage represents.

Consider the layers of meaning:

```text
Raw ADC value   →   Voltage at the pin   →   Physical quantity
   (a number)         (needs the scaling)      (needs a sensor and calibration)
```

- The raw value tells you how far along the ADC's range the input was.
- Converting it to volts needs the ADC's scaling, which as we saw is only approximately known.
- Converting the voltage to a physical quantity such as temperature or light level needs to know **how the sensor's voltage relates to that quantity**, and that relationship must be checked (or **calibrated**) against a trusted reference.

With a potentiometer, the "physical quantity" is the *knob position*. A raw value of 32,000 means "about halfway", provided the potentiometer is linear and the ADC behaves roughly linearly. It does **not** mean an exact angle of rotation in degrees unless you calibrate it.

> **Limitation to remember.** Do not read an ADC number as a temperature, a light level or a pressure just because it changes when the physical quantity changes. Until you know the sensor's relationship and have calibrated it, the number only tells you that something changed and roughly in which direction. Sensor-specific analog readings, and how to make them meaningful, come in the next reading.

### Mapping a Reading to a Useful Range

Even without calibration, you can map a raw reading onto a useful range for your program. For example, to turn the knob's reading into a percentage:

```python
def scale(value, in_min, in_max, out_min, out_max):
    """Map value from one range to another."""
    fraction = (value - in_min) / (in_max - in_min)
    return out_min + fraction * (out_max - out_min)

percent = scale(pot.value, 0, 65535, 0, 100)
```

If you measured that your board's readings really span from, say, 200 to 64,000 (example values), use those numbers as `in_min` and `in_max`, so that the *actual* ends of your knob map to 0% and 100%. That is a simple form of **calibration**: using measurements from your own system, instead of assumptions, to define the mapping.

You will often also want to **limit** the result, so noise at the ends does not push it outside the range:

```python
percent = max(0, min(100, percent))     # clamp between 0 and 100
```

## From an Analog Reading to a Program Decision

Now we have a number that follows the knob. The final step is to *use* it: to let the reading influence what the program does, and so what the outputs do.

### Threshold-Based Decisions

The simplest use is a **threshold**: a value that divides the range into "below" and "above". Comparing an analog reading with a threshold turns it into a yes-or-no decision, which is a digital decision built on an analog signal.

```python
import time
import board
import analogio
import digitalio

pot = analogio.AnalogIn(board.CHOSEN_ANALOG_PIN)

led = digitalio.DigitalInOut(board.CHOSEN_LED_PIN)
led.direction = digitalio.Direction.OUTPUT

THRESHOLD = 32768            # about the middle of the range

while True:
    if pot.value > THRESHOLD:
        led.value = True
    else:
        led.value = False
    time.sleep(0.05)
```

Turn the knob past the middle, and the LED lights. Turn it back, and it goes out. Here, at last, is the complete path from the start of the reading:

```text
Knob position  →  Voltage on the wiper  →  ADC  →  Number (0–65535)  →  Comparison with threshold  →  Output pin  →  LED
   physical          analog signal                    digital value           program logic                            actuator
```

Compare this with the button → LED program from earlier in this module. Both make an output depend on an input. With the button, the input had two possible values, so the "decision" was almost built in. With the knob, the input has thousands of possible values, and *you* choose where to draw the line. That choice is the design.

> **Try it: Compare digital and analog control.**
> 1. Run the button-controlled LED program. Then run the potentiometer-controlled LED program above.
> 2. **Predict** how each behaves as the input changes slowly. Then test.
> 3. Change `THRESHOLD` to a quarter of the range (16384), and to three quarters (49152). Where must the knob be for the LED to light in each case?
> 4. What information does the program use from the button? From the knob? What does the threshold program *throw away*?

### Making a Threshold Steady: Hysteresis

Try the threshold program with the knob very close to the threshold. The LED may flicker on and off. The reading is noisy, as we will see, and it wobbles slightly above and below the threshold value. Each crossing changes the output.

You met this idea in the first module, in the street-light discussion, and the cure is the same as the debouncing idea from the previous reading: do not respond to every tiny change. Use **two thresholds**, one to switch on and a lower one to switch off. The gap between them is called **hysteresis**.

```text
value ▲
      │                       ┌─── switch ON when the value rises above this
 high │- - - - - - - - - - - -┼─ - - - - - - - - -
      │                       │
      │        the value can wobble here without changing the output
      │                       │
 low  │- - - - - - - - - - - -┼─ - - - - - - - - -
      │                       └─── switch OFF only when it falls below this
      └──────────────────────────────────────────► time
```

```python
ON_ABOVE = 34000             # example values: turn on above this
OFF_BELOW = 30000            # turn off below this

alarm_on = False

while True:
    value = pot.value

    if not alarm_on and value > ON_ABOVE:
        alarm_on = True
    elif alarm_on and value < OFF_BELOW:
        alarm_on = False

    led.value = alarm_on
    time.sleep(0.05)
```

The variable `alarm_on` is the program's **memory** of what state it is in. Between the two thresholds, the output keeps its previous state, so small wobbles do nothing.

> **Try it: Test the hysteresis.** Set the knob close to the threshold and gently wobble it. Compare this program with the simple single-threshold version. What differs? What happens if the gap between the thresholds is very small? Very large?

### Ranges and Zones

A single threshold splits the range in two. Several thresholds split it into **zones**, each with its own behaviour. This is how one analog input can control several different outputs, or different behaviours of one output.

Here is a program that divides the knob's range into four zones, and shows the zone by lighting 0, 1, 2 or 3 of three LEDs (the `leds` list from earlier in this reading):

```python
import time
import board
import analogio
import digitalio

pot = analogio.AnalogIn(board.CHOSEN_ANALOG_PIN)

led_pins = [board.CHOSEN_LED_PIN_1, board.CHOSEN_LED_PIN_2, board.CHOSEN_LED_PIN_3]
leds = []
for pin in led_pins:
    led = digitalio.DigitalInOut(pin)
    led.direction = digitalio.Direction.OUTPUT
    leds.append(led)

while True:
    fraction = pot.value / 65535              # 0.0 to 1.0
    level = min(int(fraction * 4), 3)         # zone 0, 1, 2 or 3

    for i in range(3):
        leds[i].value = (i < level)           # LED i is on if the zone is above it

    time.sleep(0.05)
```

The interesting line is `level = min(int(fraction * 4), 3)`. Multiplying the fraction by 4 gives a number from 0 to 4. `int(...)` drops the decimal part, so values in the first quarter become 0, the second quarter 1 and so on. `min(..., 3)` keeps the very top of the range in zone 3, because a fraction of exactly 1.0 would otherwise give 4.

Zones are also a good way to combine outputs. For instance:

| Knob position | LED | Buzzer |
|---|---|---|
| Lower third | Off | Silent |
| Middle third | Slow blink | Silent |
| Upper third | Fast blink | Short beeps |

> **Try it: Modify the ranges.**
> - Change the number of zones from four to five. Which lines change?
> - Use the decision-table idea from earlier: choose three zones and give each a different LED and buzzer behaviour. Write the table first, predict what you will see at each knob position, then build it.
> - Where in your program do you decide which zone applies, and where do you act on that decision? Why is it useful to keep those two steps apart?

### The Complete System

Putting it all together, the system you can now build is:

```text
Potentiometer → ESP32-S3 → LED / Buzzer

  knob  ─►  analog voltage  ─►  [ ADC ]  ─►  number  ─►  program logic  ─►  GPIO output  ─►  LED / buzzer
                                                          (thresholds,
                                                           zones, memory)
```

This is the Sense → Compute → Actuate model in its analog form. The *sensing* end has changed from a two-state signal to a continuous one, and the *actuating* end is the digital output you already know. The middle, your program, is where the number becomes a decision.

## When Analog Readings Are Unstable or Unexpected

Analog inputs are more delicate than digital ones. A digital input asks a coarse question, "is it above or below a threshold?", and tolerates small errors. An analog input reports a number, so small errors show up in the number. Here are the common surprises, and how to reason about them.

### Noise

If you hold the knob perfectly still and print the readings, you will probably see them **jitter** by a few counts, or more. This is **noise**: small, random variations from electrical interference, the ADC itself and the power supply. It is normal.

Two techniques reduce its effect:

**Averaging.** Take several samples and use their average. Random errors tend to cancel out:

```python
def read_average(pin, samples=16):
    total = 0
    for _ in range(samples):
        total = total + pin.value
    return total // samples          # integer average

value = read_average(pot)
```

The trade-off is a slower response, since each reading takes more samples. (Espressif's documentation for the ESP32-S3 ADC also recommends multisampling to reduce noise.)

**A small capacitor.** Espressif's documentation suggests connecting a small ceramic capacitor, for example 100 nF, between the ADC input and ground to reduce noise. You met the capacitor as a small reservoir that smooths sudden changes. Here it smooths tiny, fast fluctuations on the input. The trade-off is that the input responds a little more slowly to genuine changes.

### A Troubleshooting Guide

Apply the same order of reasoning you have used before: observe, isolate, measure, hypothesise, test.

| What you observe | Likely causes | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| The reading is stuck near 0 | Wiper not connected, wiring to wrong terminal, ground connected but 3.3 V missing | Measure the voltage at the wiper with a multimeter. Check the outer terminals |
| The reading is stuck near the maximum | Wiper connected to the supply, 3.3 V on the wrong terminal | Measure the wiper voltage and check the wiring |
| The reading jumps around when nothing is touched | The analog pin is **floating** (nothing connected), or the wiper has a poor connection | Just like a floating digital input, an unconnected analog pin picks up noise. Check the wiring and connectors |
| A small jitter of a few counts | Ordinary noise | Averaging, or a small capacitor. Not a fault |
| The reading never reaches the very top or bottom | Limits of the ADC's usable range and non-linearity near the ends | Measure the voltage with a multimeter and compare it. Use your measured minimum and maximum |
| Turning the knob has no effect on the reading | Pin is not the one you wired, or the pin is not analog-capable | Check the pin name, and that the pin has an ADC connection. Read any error message |
| Reading changes in the wrong direction | Outer terminals swapped | Swap them, or invert the value in code |
| The output flickers near a threshold | Noise combined with a single threshold | Add hysteresis, or average |
| Readings behave oddly once Wi-Fi is used | On ESP32-family chips, pins tied to one of the ADC units can be unreliable while Wi-Fi is active | Check your board's documentation for which pins to prefer. This matters in later modules |

> **Assumption.** The last row describes a known limitation of ESP32-family chips, documented for the ADC that shares hardware with the Wi-Fi radio. The details differ by chip and by software version, so check your board's documentation before relying on any particular pin once Wi-Fi comes into your projects.

A very useful diagnostic is to **print raw values, voltages and a multimeter reading side by side**. If the multimeter shows a steady voltage while the program's numbers jitter, the noise is in the measurement chain. If the multimeter shows the voltage moving, the source is unstable. Isolating *where* the instability appears tells you where to look.

# Putting It All Together

## The Complete Signal Path

Here is the full chain from the physical world to a physical response, with each link named:

```text
Physical quantity (knob position)
        │
        ▼
Analog signal (wiper voltage)          ← a continuous voltage, 0 V to about 3.3 V
        │
        ▼
ADC (analog-to-digital conversion)     ← measures and rounds, at 12-bit resolution
        │
        ▼
Digital value (0–65535 in CircuitPython)   ← a raw number, not yet a physical unit
        │
        ▼
Program logic (thresholds, zones, memory)  ← the decision
        │
        ▼
Actuator (LED, buzzer)                 ← the physical response
```

The mental model to hold on to: **the physical world is often continuous, the microcontroller works with numbers, and the ADC is the bridge between them.**
