<div style="background:linear-gradient(90deg,#dbeafe 0%,#ffffff 100%);border-left:6px solid #1e3a8a;border-radius:6px;padding:10px 16px;margin:34px 0 16px 0;font-size:22px;font-weight:700;color:#1e3a8a;">The Sensor From the Last Tutorial Is Not a Decision</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">Ask a class to design a step counter and most students will reach for the accelerometer they used last time. Ask them to detect a person in a room and they reach for the PIR sensor from the kit. Sometimes that is right. Often it is just familiar.</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">A sensor is not chosen by what you have used before. It is chosen by answering a harder question first: <strong>what physical quantity actually tells you the thing you care about?</strong> "Measure heart rate" sounds like a specification, but it is not. Heart rate can be sensed through light, through electrical signals, through pressure, and each of those works differently on a wrist, in a lab and during a run. The right choice depends on where the sensor is worn, what the user will put up with, and which kind of mistake you can afford.</div>

<div style="background:linear-gradient(90deg,#dbeafe 0%,#ffffff 100%);border-left:6px solid #1e3a8a;border-radius:6px;padding:10px 16px;margin:34px 0 16px 0;font-size:22px;font-weight:700;color:#1e3a8a;">What You Will Be Able to Do After This Reading</div>

<ul style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;padding-left:22px;"><li style="margin:6px 0;">​<strong>Identify</strong> the physical quantity that genuinely indicates what your product needs to know.</li><li style="margin:6px 0;">​<strong>Compare</strong> sensor families on what they measure, their output and how they fail.</li><li style="margin:6px 0;">​<strong>Apply</strong> a selection framework: contact, range, response time, environment, output, power, cost, and which error is more expensive.</li><li style="margin:6px 0;">​<strong>Justify</strong> each sensor in your design, with a rejected alternative and a stated reason.</li><li style="margin:6px 0;">​<strong>Produce</strong> a sensor selection matrix for your own product.</li></ul>

<div style="background:linear-gradient(90deg,#dbeafe 0%,#ffffff 100%);border-left:6px solid #1e3a8a;border-radius:6px;padding:10px 16px;margin:34px 0 16px 0;font-size:22px;font-weight:700;color:#1e3a8a;">Part 2 — A Selection Framework</div>

<div style="background:linear-gradient(90deg,#dbeafe 0%,#ffffff 100%);border-left:6px solid #1e3a8a;border-radius:6px;padding:10px 16px;margin:34px 0 16px 0;font-size:22px;font-weight:700;color:#1e3a8a;">The Questions, in Order</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">Work through these questions for every quantity your product must sense. Record the answers; they become your selection matrix.</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:14px 0 8px 0;">1. <strong>What physical quantity genuinely indicates what you care about?</strong> Name the measurand and every candidate proxy.</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:14px 0 8px 0;">2. <strong>Contact or non-contact?</strong> Must the sensor touch the thing, and will the user accept that?</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:14px 0 8px 0;">3. <strong>Range and resolution.</strong> What is the smallest change that matters, and the largest value you must read?</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:14px 0 8px 0;">4. <strong>Response time.</strong> How quickly must a change be noticed?</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:14px 0 8px 0;">5. <strong>Environment.</strong> Heat, sweat, dust, light, vibration: what will the sensor face (A0)?</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:14px 0 8px 0;">6. <strong>Output type.</strong> Will it fit the interfaces and pins you have? (You decide these in B1 and B3.)</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:14px 0 8px 0;">7. <strong>Power.</strong> How much, and can it be switched off between readings? (B3 adds it up.)</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:14px 0 8px 0;">8. <strong>Cost</strong> at your quantities (B4).</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:14px 0 8px 0;">9. <strong>Which error is more expensive: a false positive or a false negative?</strong></div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">The last question changes decisions most. A <strong>false positive</strong> reports something that did not happen: a step that was really a clap. A <strong>false negative</strong> misses something that did happen: a heartbeat not detected. For a step counter, both are mild. For a fall detector, a missed fall is far worse than a false alarm. Decide which matters more before choosing: it tells you which sensor weakness you cannot accept.</div>

<div style="background:linear-gradient(90deg,#dbeafe 0%,#ffffff 100%);border-left:6px solid #1e3a8a;border-radius:6px;padding:10px 16px;margin:34px 0 16px 0;font-size:22px;font-weight:700;color:#1e3a8a;">Worked Example 1: Heart Rate From a Wrist, Without the Wearer Doing Anything</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">​<strong>The requirement:</strong> "A wrist-worn device must measure heart rate without the wearer doing anything."</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">​<strong>Step 1: Name the measurand and the candidates.</strong> The measurand is the heartbeat. Four ways to sense it:</div>

<table style="margin:16px auto;border-collapse:collapse;"><thead><tr><th style="background:#2563eb;color:#ffffff;font-weight:700;padding:10px 28px;border:1px solid #1e40af;text-align:center;">Candidate</th><th style="background:#2563eb;color:#ffffff;font-weight:700;padding:10px 28px;border:1px solid #1e40af;text-align:center;">What it measures</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td style="padding:9px 28px;border:1px solid #bfdbfe;background:#eff6ff;text-align:center;">Optical PPG</td><td style="padding:9px 28px;border:1px solid #bfdbfe;background:#eff6ff;text-align:center;">Changes in light reflected from blood under the skin</td></tr><tr><td style="padding:9px 28px;border:1px solid #bfdbfe;background:#eff6ff;text-align:center;">ECG electrodes</td><td style="padding:9px 28px;border:1px solid #bfdbfe;background:#eff6ff;text-align:center;">The heart's electrical activity, between two points on the body</td></tr><tr><td style="padding:9px 28px;border:1px solid #bfdbfe;background:#eff6ff;text-align:center;">Chest strap</td><td style="padding:9px 28px;border:1px solid #bfdbfe;background:#eff6ff;text-align:center;">ECG, from electrodes held against the chest</td></tr><tr><td style="padding:9px 28px;border:1px solid #bfdbfe;background:#eff6ff;text-align:center;">Piezo pulse sensor</td><td style="padding:9px 28px;border:1px solid #bfdbfe;background:#eff6ff;text-align:center;">Pressure from the pulse at an artery</td></tr></tbody></table>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">​<strong>Step 2: Test each against the requirement's conditions.</strong> The requirement contains two conditions: wrist-worn and without the wearer doing anything.</div>

<ul style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;padding-left:22px;"><li style="margin:6px 0;">​<strong>ECG</strong> gives a clean signal, but it needs electrodes touching the body at two separated points, with the heart between them. A single wrist is one point. Watches that record an ECG ask the wearer to touch the watch with the other hand, which breaks "without doing anything". <strong>Rejected: needs a deliberate action.</strong></li><li style="margin:6px 0;">​<strong>Chest strap</strong> is accurate and continuous. It is also on the chest, not the wrist, and for a hostel student wearing it all day it is simply unacceptable. <strong>Rejected: user tolerance.</strong></li><li style="margin:6px 0;">​<strong>Piezo pulse sensor</strong> needs to sit firmly over an artery. On a wrist that moves and a strap that shifts, it loses the pulse easily. <strong>Rejected: placement too fragile.</strong></li><li style="margin:6px 0;">​<strong>Optical PPG</strong> works passively from the back of the wrist. <strong>Kept</strong>, with its weaknesses written down.</li></ul>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">​<strong>Step 3: State how the chosen sensor fails.</strong> Published research gives this real numbers. A study comparing six consumer and research wearables against a medical ECG found heart-rate error during activity was on average about 30% higher than at rest [1]. It also describes <strong>signal crossover</strong>: the sensor can lock on to the rhythm of repetitive motion, such as walking, and report that as the heart rate [1]. Whether skin tone affects wrist PPG accuracy is still debated. That study found no significant difference across skin tones [1], while a published response argued its sample of the darkest skin tones was too small to be sure [2].</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">esp\_watch's responses (the enclosure and real-life testing are not finished yet):</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">​<strong>Check.</strong> <strong>"Measure heart rate" was never a specification.</strong> The quantity actually sensed (reflected light), the wearing position (one wrist), the user's tolerance (no chest strap, no second hand) and the failure modes (motion, fit, light) decided it. A gym machine with metal hand grips would reasonably choose ECG.</div>

<div style="background:#eff6ff;border-left:4px solid #2563eb;border-radius:8px;padding:12px 16px;margin:16px 0;text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;"><div style="font-weight:700;margin-bottom:3px;color:#1e40af;">Try it: Change one condition.</div><div>Rewrite the requirement as "A device must measure heart rate accurately during a 30-minute run."</div><div>1. <strong>Predict.</strong> Does PPG still win?</div><div>2. <strong>Do.</strong> Re-run Step 2 with the new condition. Give each candidate a verdict and a reason.</div><div>3. <strong>Explain.</strong> Which candidate's rejection reason changed? What does that tell you about where the decision really came from?</div></div>

<div style="background:linear-gradient(90deg,#dbeafe 0%,#ffffff 100%);border-left:6px solid #1e3a8a;border-radius:6px;padding:10px 16px;margin:34px 0 16px 0;font-size:22px;font-weight:700;color:#1e3a8a;">Worked Example 2: Three Sensing Jobs in One Machine</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">This example is deliberately far from wearables.</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">​<strong>The requirement:</strong> "A small conveyor counts bottles going past, checks whether each has a cap, and measures how fast the belt is moving."</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">​<strong>Step 1: Split it into separate sensing problems.</strong> These are three different jobs:</div>

<table style="margin:16px auto;border-collapse:collapse;"><thead><tr><th style="background:#2563eb;color:#ffffff;font-weight:700;padding:10px 28px;border:1px solid #1e40af;text-align:center;">Job</th><th style="background:#2563eb;color:#ffffff;font-weight:700;padding:10px 28px;border:1px solid #1e40af;text-align:center;">Measurand</th><th style="background:#2563eb;color:#ffffff;font-weight:700;padding:10px 28px;border:1px solid #1e40af;text-align:center;">Kind of problem</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td style="padding:9px 28px;border:1px solid #bfdbfe;background:#eff6ff;text-align:center;">Count bottles</td><td style="padding:9px 28px;border:1px solid #bfdbfe;background:#eff6ff;text-align:center;">A bottle passing a point</td><td style="padding:9px 28px;border:1px solid #bfdbfe;background:#eff6ff;text-align:center;">Detect a discrete event</td></tr><tr><td style="padding:9px 28px;border:1px solid #bfdbfe;background:#eff6ff;text-align:center;">Check for a cap</td><td style="padding:9px 28px;border:1px solid #bfdbfe;background:#eff6ff;text-align:center;">A feature at the top of each bottle</td><td style="padding:9px 28px;border:1px solid #bfdbfe;background:#eff6ff;text-align:center;">Inspect a feature</td></tr><tr><td style="padding:9px 28px;border:1px solid #bfdbfe;background:#eff6ff;text-align:center;">Belt speed</td><td style="padding:9px 28px;border:1px solid #bfdbfe;background:#eff6ff;text-align:center;">Rate of belt movement</td><td style="padding:9px 28px;border:1px solid #bfdbfe;background:#eff6ff;text-align:center;">Measure a rate</td></tr></tbody></table>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:14px 0 8px 0;font-weight:700;">Step 2: Pick candidates for each.</div>

<table style="margin:16px auto;border-collapse:collapse;"><thead><tr><th style="background:#2563eb;color:#ffffff;font-weight:700;padding:10px 28px;border:1px solid #1e40af;text-align:center;">Job</th><th style="background:#2563eb;color:#ffffff;font-weight:700;padding:10px 28px;border:1px solid #1e40af;text-align:center;">Candidates</th><th style="background:#2563eb;color:#ffffff;font-weight:700;padding:10px 28px;border:1px solid #1e40af;text-align:center;">Choice and reason</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td style="padding:9px 28px;border:1px solid #bfdbfe;background:#eff6ff;text-align:center;">Count bottles</td><td style="padding:9px 28px;border:1px solid #bfdbfe;background:#eff6ff;text-align:center;">Infrared break-beam; reflective infrared; camera</td><td style="padding:9px 28px;border:1px solid #bfdbfe;background:#eff6ff;text-align:center;">​<strong>Break-beam</strong>: simple, fast, cheap, and a bottle reliably blocks it. A camera is overkill for "something passed".</td></tr><tr><td style="padding:9px 28px;border:1px solid #bfdbfe;background:#eff6ff;text-align:center;">Check for a cap</td><td style="padding:9px 28px;border:1px solid #bfdbfe;background:#eff6ff;text-align:center;">Reflective sensor at cap height; inductive proximity sensor (metal caps only); camera</td><td style="padding:9px 28px;border:1px solid #bfdbfe;background:#eff6ff;text-align:center;">​<strong>Reflective sensor at cap height</strong>, if caps and bottle necks look different to it. If caps are metal, an <strong>inductive sensor</strong> ignores bottle colour entirely.</td></tr><tr><td style="padding:9px 28px;border:1px solid #bfdbfe;background:#eff6ff;text-align:center;">Belt speed</td><td style="padding:9px 28px;border:1px solid #bfdbfe;background:#eff6ff;text-align:center;">Encoder on a roller; magnet on the roller with a Hall-effect sensor; timing bottles between two break-beams</td><td style="padding:9px 28px;border:1px solid #bfdbfe;background:#eff6ff;text-align:center;">​<strong>Magnet and Hall sensor</strong>: one pulse per turn, simple and non-contact. Timing bottles fails when there are gaps in the line.</td></tr></tbody></table>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">​<strong>Step 3: Which error is more expensive?</strong> For counting, a missed bottle or a double count is a small bookkeeping error. For cap checking, a <strong>false negative</strong>, an uncapped bottle passed as capped, reaches a customer. So the cap check is where money should go: a better sensor, a second sensor, or a check that fails safe.</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">​<strong>Check.</strong> The cheapest sensor, a break-beam, is enough for counting and hopeless for cap checking, even in the same machine. Each job gets its own measurand, its own sensor and its own tolerance for error.</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">​<strong>1.</strong> A fall detector for elderly people must alert a carer. Which error should the sensor choice work hardest to avoid, and why?</div>

<ul style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;padding-left:22px;"><li style="margin:6px 0;">A. False positives, because false alarms annoy the carer.</li><li style="margin:6px 0;">B. False negatives, because a real fall that is missed could leave someone without help.</li><li style="margin:6px 0;">C. Neither; both are equally bad.</li><li style="margin:6px 0;">D. Whichever is cheaper to fix in firmware.</li></ul>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">Answer</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">​<strong>B.</strong> A missed fall has a far higher cost than a false alarm. <strong>A</strong> is a real concern, but a secondary one: it is usually handled with a "cancel" button, not by accepting missed falls. <strong>C</strong> ignores the consequences. <strong>D</strong> puts implementation convenience ahead of the requirement.</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">​<strong>2.</strong> A room-occupancy system uses a PIR sensor. It reports the room empty while a student sits studying. What is the best explanation?</div>

<ul style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;padding-left:22px;"><li style="margin:6px 0;">A. The PIR sensor is faulty.</li><li style="margin:6px 0;">B. PIR detects a changing heat pattern, so a still person is invisible to it; the proxy (movement) does not match the measurand (presence).</li><li style="margin:6px 0;">C. The room is too warm.</li><li style="margin:6px 0;">D. The sensor needs a faster interface.</li></ul>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">Answer</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">​<strong>B.</strong> PIR responds to movement of heat, not to presence. That is a known failure mode of the family, not a fault. <strong>A</strong> blames the part for doing exactly what it measures. <strong>C</strong> can reduce sensitivity, but it does not explain a still person being missed. <strong>D</strong> is unrelated to what the sensor detects.</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">​<strong>3.</strong> A wrist PPG sensor reports 120 bpm while the wearer walks at 2 steps per second. A chest-strap reference shows 95 bpm. What is the most likely explanation?</div>

<ul style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;padding-left:22px;"><li style="margin:6px 0;">A. The chest strap is wrong.</li><li style="margin:6px 0;">B. Signal crossover: the PPG has locked on to the walking rhythm (2 steps per second = 120 per minute) instead of the heartbeat.</li><li style="margin:6px 0;">C. The wearer's skin tone.</li><li style="margin:6px 0;">D. The I²C bus is too slow.</li></ul>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">Answer</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">​<strong>B.</strong> Two steps per second is 120 per minute, matching the wrong reading: signal crossover [1]. The accelerometer can catch this: if heart rate equals step rate, be suspicious. <strong>A</strong>: chest straps are the usual reference. <strong>C</strong> does not explain a match to the step rate. <strong>D</strong> would lose data, not create a false rhythm.</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">​<strong>4.</strong> A bottling line's sensor passes uncapped bottles about once in 500. Where should improvements focus?</div>

<ul style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;padding-left:22px;"><li style="margin:6px 0;">A. The bottle counter, because counting errors add up.</li><li style="margin:6px 0;">B. The cap check, because a false negative (an uncapped bottle passed as capped) reaches customers.</li><li style="margin:6px 0;">C. The belt-speed sensor.</li><li style="margin:6px 0;">D. All three sensors equally.</li></ul>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">Answer</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">​<strong>B.</strong> The expensive error is the one that leaves the factory. <strong>A</strong> matters for bookkeeping, but a miscount does not harm a customer. <strong>C</strong> has nothing to do with caps. <strong>D</strong> spreads effort evenly instead of where the costlier error is.</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">​<strong>5.</strong> A team wants to show room temperature on a watch, using a temperature sensor on the watch's circuit board. What is the main problem?</div>

<ul style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;padding-left:22px;"><li style="margin:6px 0;">A. Temperature sensors need SPI.</li><li style="margin:6px 0;">B. The sensor measures the board's own temperature, which is warmed by the wrist, the battery and the electronics, not the room.</li><li style="margin:6px 0;">C. The display cannot show decimals.</li><li style="margin:6px 0;">D. There is no problem.</li></ul>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">Answer</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">​<strong>B.</strong> The proxy is the board's temperature, and on a wrist it is dominated by body heat and self-heating. <strong>A</strong> is false; many temperature sensors use I²C. <strong>C</strong> is irrelevant. <strong>D</strong> ignores what the sensor actually measures.</div>
