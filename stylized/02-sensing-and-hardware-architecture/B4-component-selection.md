<div style="background:linear-gradient(135deg,#eff6ff 0%,#dbeafe 60%,#bfdbfe 100%);border:2px solid #2563eb;padding:28px 32px;border-radius:12px;margin-bottom:20px;"><div style="margin:0;font-size:32px;font-weight:700;letter-spacing:-0.5px;color:#1e3a8a;line-height:1.25;">B4 — Component Selection: Modules or Discrete ICs?</div></div>

<div style="background:linear-gradient(90deg,#dbeafe 0%,#ffffff 100%);border-left:6px solid #1e3a8a;border-radius:6px;padding:10px 16px;margin:34px 0 16px 0;font-size:22px;font-weight:700;color:#1e3a8a;">Choosing Real Parts, and Reading the Documents That Decide Them</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">​<strong>Course:</strong> C3 — From Problem Statement to Manufacturable Design <strong>Module:</strong> 2 — Sensing and Hardware Architecture <strong>Time:</strong> ~1.5 hours · <strong>You will produce:</strong> a module-versus-IC decision table, a comparison matrix for three candidate parts, and a preliminary BOM</div>

---

<div style="background:linear-gradient(90deg,#dbeafe 0%,#ffffff 100%);border-left:6px solid #1e3a8a;border-radius:6px;padding:10px 16px;margin:34px 0 16px 0;font-size:22px;font-weight:700;color:#1e3a8a;">The Same Sensor, Three Different Decisions</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">Search any Indian electronics shop for "MAX30102" and you will find a small, ready-made board for under ₹200, often in two colours. Search a component distributor for the same name and you will find the bare chip, a tiny 5.6 × 3.3 mm optical package, for more than the whole ready-made board costs.</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">Which should you use? The ready-made board is easier to design with and cheaper today. The bare chip is thinner and gives you full control. And the two colours of ready-made board are not the same: on the reference watch, one of them made the whole I²C bus fail.</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">Every line of a bill of materials involves this decision: use a <strong>module</strong> someone else designed, or place the <strong>chip</strong> yourself.</div>

<div style="background:linear-gradient(90deg,#dbeafe 0%,#ffffff 100%);border-left:6px solid #1e3a8a;border-radius:6px;padding:10px 16px;margin:34px 0 16px 0;font-size:22px;font-weight:700;color:#1e3a8a;">What You Will Be Able to Do After This Reading</div>

<ul style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;padding-left:22px;"><li style="margin:6px 0;">​<strong>Decide</strong>, per part, between a module and a discrete IC, and justify the decision against your spec.</li><li style="margin:6px 0;">​<strong>Extract</strong> absolute maximum ratings, recommended operating conditions and the application circuit from a datasheet.</li><li style="margin:6px 0;">​<strong>Distinguish</strong> what a module's listing tells you from what the chip's datasheet tells you, and find the gap between them.</li><li style="margin:6px 0;">​<strong>Compare</strong> three candidate parts in a weighted matrix.</li><li style="margin:6px 0;">​<strong>Produce</strong> a preliminary BOM with part numbers, suppliers, prices, stock and lifecycle status.</li></ul>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">Part 1 had you wire modules and read pinouts. New here: choosing parts, reading datasheets for their limits, and recording price, stock and lifecycle.</div>

---

<div style="background:linear-gradient(90deg,#dbeafe 0%,#ffffff 100%);border-left:6px solid #1e3a8a;border-radius:6px;padding:10px 16px;margin:34px 0 16px 0;font-size:22px;font-weight:700;color:#1e3a8a;">Module or Chip?</div>

<div style="background:linear-gradient(90deg,#dbeafe 0%,#ffffff 100%);border-left:6px solid #1e3a8a;border-radius:6px;padding:10px 16px;margin:34px 0 16px 0;font-size:22px;font-weight:700;color:#1e3a8a;">What You Gain and What You Give Up</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">A <strong>breakout module</strong> is a small board carrying a chip plus the parts it needs: regulators, capacitors, pull-up resistors and sometimes level shifters. A <strong>discrete IC</strong> is the chip alone, and you design the supporting circuit yourself.</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">The last-but-one row catches students out. A module from a marketplace seller has no datasheet of its own, no revision history and no promise that next month's batch is the same design. The chip inside has all three. You may find that a module you relied on has quietly changed its pull-ups, its regulator or its chip.</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">A common belief is that modules are a beginner's shortcut and "real" products use chips. Many real products do move to chips, but usually at volume, once the design is proven. For a first design, especially one that will be hand-soldered, a module is often the correct engineering choice. What matters is that you can say why.</div>

<div style="background:linear-gradient(90deg,#dbeafe 0%,#ffffff 100%);border-left:6px solid #1e3a8a;border-radius:6px;padding:10px 16px;margin:34px 0 16px 0;font-size:22px;font-weight:700;color:#1e3a8a;">Deciding Per Part</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">Make the decision separately for each part, because the answer is often different. Ask four questions, each tied to your spec:</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:14px 0 8px 0;">1. <strong>Does it fit?</strong> Check your size envelope from A0, especially height.</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:14px 0 8px 0;">2. <strong>Can we assemble it?</strong> Leadless and fine-pitch chips need reflow soldering. If your build will be hand-soldered, that decides a lot.</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:14px 0 8px 0;">3. <strong>What does the application circuit need?</strong> Extra supply voltages, precise analog parts or an antenna all push towards a module.</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:14px 0 8px 0;">4. <strong>What happens at our quantity?</strong> Check prices at 1 and 10.</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">esp\_watch chose modules for every active part, and recorded it as its design approach: the microcontroller, both sensors and the display are all pre-made modules on a custom carrier board. No discrete ICs were placed. Assembly will be done by hand: everything is through-hole or large surface-mount, so no assembly service is needed. The reasons in the table below are reconstructed from the design as built.</div>

<div style="background:linear-gradient(90deg,#dbeafe 0%,#ffffff 100%);border-left:6px solid #1e3a8a;border-radius:6px;padding:10px 16px;margin:34px 0 16px 0;font-size:22px;font-weight:700;color:#1e3a8a;">Reading a Datasheet for Its Limits</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">For selection, you need the sections that tell you whether a part will survive and work in your design.</div>

<div style="background:linear-gradient(90deg,#dbeafe 0%,#ffffff 100%);border-left:6px solid #1e3a8a;border-radius:6px;padding:10px 16px;margin:34px 0 16px 0;font-size:22px;font-weight:700;color:#1e3a8a;">Absolute Maximum vs Recommended Operating Conditions</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">These two tables are easy to confuse, and confusing them destroys parts.</div>

<ul style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;padding-left:22px;"><li style="margin:6px 0;">​<strong>Absolute maximum ratings</strong> are the limits beyond which the part may be permanently damaged. They are not operating conditions. A part run just below its absolute maximum should survive, but nothing says it will work.</li><li style="margin:6px 0;">​<strong>Recommended operating conditions</strong> (or the electrical characteristics' supply range) are where the part is guaranteed to work as specified. Design within these, with margin.</li></ul>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">Here is the MAX30102 heart-rate chip [1]:</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">Read that first row carefully. The chip's main supply must be <strong>1.8 V</strong>, and 3.3 V would exceed its absolute maximum. Yet the LED supply wants 3.3 V, and the I²C pins tolerate up to 6 V. So a MAX30102 needs two supply voltages, and a module built around it must generate 1.8 V somewhere on the board.</div>

<div style="background:linear-gradient(90deg,#dbeafe 0%,#ffffff 100%);border-left:6px solid #1e3a8a;border-radius:6px;padding:10px 16px;margin:34px 0 16px 0;font-size:22px;font-weight:700;color:#1e3a8a;">The Application Circuit</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">Most datasheets include a <strong>typical application circuit</strong>: the manufacturer's recommended way to connect the chip. It lists every supporting part a bare-chip design needs. For the MAX30102 [1], that means a 1.8 V regulator, decoupling, and I²C pull-ups to a voltage both sides accept.</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">The chip's I²C inputs treat anything above a low, fixed threshold (VIH, about 1.4 V [1]) as HIGH, and tolerate up to 6 V. So a 3.3 V microcontroller can read it with pull-ups to 3.3 V. Pull-ups to 1.8 V also suit the chip, but they drag down a 3.3 V bus shared with other devices.</div>

<div style="background:linear-gradient(90deg,#dbeafe 0%,#ffffff 100%);border-left:6px solid #1e3a8a;border-radius:6px;padding:10px 16px;margin:34px 0 16px 0;font-size:22px;font-weight:700;color:#1e3a8a;">The Module's Listing Is Not the Chip's Datasheet</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">A module's product page and the chip's datasheet are <strong>different documents saying different things</strong>. The datasheet describes the chip. It cannot tell you what regulator the module maker fitted, what the pull-ups connect to, or whether the design changed last month.</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">esp\_watch's two heart-rate modules carry the same chip. The green one ties its I²C lines to 1.8 V and broke the shared bus. The black one uses 3.3 V and worked (B3). Only the module's schematic, or a measurement, could show the difference.</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">Even the listing for the black module needs care. Robu's page describes its communication interface voltage as "1.8, 3.3V, 5V (optional)" [2], which suggests the level is selectable on the board. A module that can be set to 1.8 V may arrive set to 1.8 V. Before you accept a module, find out how its pull-up voltage is set, and write it in your interface table.</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">For a labelled photo of a MAX30102 breakout module, see Last Minute Engineers' pinout guide: https://lastminuteengineers.com/wp-content/uploads/arduino/MAX30102-Module-Pinout.png</div>

<div style="background:linear-gradient(90deg,#dbeafe 0%,#ffffff 100%);border-left:6px solid #1e3a8a;border-radius:6px;padding:10px 16px;margin:34px 0 16px 0;font-size:22px;font-weight:700;color:#1e3a8a;">Package and Footprint</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">Finally, the <strong>package</strong> section tells you the part's physical size and how it is soldered. The bare MAX30102 is a 14-pin optical module measuring 5.6 × 3.3 × 1.55 mm, with integrated cover glass [1]. Its pads are underneath the package, so it cannot be soldered with an iron. You will return to packages and footprints in C2; for selection, just ask whether you can assemble it.</div>
