<div style="background:linear-gradient(90deg,#dbeafe 0%,#ffffff 100%);border-left:6px solid #1e3a8a;border-radius:6px;padding:10px 16px;margin:34px 0 16px 0;font-size:22px;font-weight:700;color:#1e3a8a;">Comparing Candidates and Writing the First BOM</div>

<div style="background:linear-gradient(90deg,#dbeafe 0%,#ffffff 100%);border-left:6px solid #1e3a8a;border-radius:6px;padding:10px 16px;margin:34px 0 16px 0;font-size:22px;font-weight:700;color:#1e3a8a;">The Comparison Matrix</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">When more than one part could do the job, compare them in a <strong>matrix</strong>: candidates as columns, criteria as rows. Take the criteria from your spec and B3's electrical architecture, not from the product page. Give each criterion a <strong>weight</strong> for how much it matters to your product, score each candidate, and multiply.</div>

<div style="background:#eff6ff;border-left:4px solid #2563eb;border-radius:8px;padding:12px 16px;margin:16px 0;text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;"><div style="font-weight:700;margin-bottom:3px;color:#1e40af;">Teaching model.</div><div>A weighted matrix makes your reasoning visible and forces you to name what matters. It does not make the decision for you. If the winner feels wrong, one of your weights is probably wrong, and finding out which is useful.</div></div>

<div style="background:linear-gradient(90deg,#dbeafe 0%,#ffffff 100%);border-left:6px solid #1e3a8a;border-radius:6px;padding:10px 16px;margin:34px 0 16px 0;font-size:22px;font-weight:700;color:#1e3a8a;">Worked Example: Three Ways to Measure Heart Rate</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">Notice also the price row. The bare chip at quantity 1 costs about seven times as much as a complete module carrying the same chip. <div style="background:linear-gradient(90deg,#dbeafe 0%,#ffffff 100%);border-left:6px solid #1e3a8a;border-radius:6px;padding:10px 16px;margin:34px 0 16px 0;font-size:22px;font-weight:700;color:#1e3a8a;">Parametric Search and Stock</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">To find candidates, use a distributor's <strong>parametric search</strong>: choose a category, then filter by the numbers that matter to you, such as supply voltage, interface, package and stock. LCSC, Mouser and DigiKey all work this way, and Indian shops such as Robu, Robocraze and Element14 India list the common modules. Filter by your requirements, not by the first result.</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">For each candidate you shortlist, record three more things:</div>

<ul style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;padding-left:22px;"><li style="margin:6px 0;">​<strong>Stock</strong>, at more than one supplier. On the day this unit was written, three of esp\_watch's four modules were out of stock at Robu [2][4][5]. A second supplier is not a luxury.</li><li style="margin:6px 0;">​<strong>Price at 1 and 10</strong>, the quantities of your prototype and funded build.</li><li style="margin:6px 0;">​<strong>Lifecycle status</strong>: whether the part is still in production. Just note what the distributor says for now; F0 teaches how to check it properly.</li></ul>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">One of esp\_watch's parts, the MPU-6050, is officially obsolete. F0 tells that story in full.</div>

<div style="background:linear-gradient(90deg,#dbeafe 0%,#ffffff 100%);border-left:6px solid #1e3a8a;border-radius:6px;padding:10px 16px;margin:34px 0 16px 0;font-size:22px;font-weight:700;color:#1e3a8a;">Applying What You Have Learned</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">​<strong>1. Build your module-versus-IC decision table.</strong> One row per active part in your B2 block diagram. Columns: part, choice, main reason (traced to a requirement or an assembly constraint), main cost.</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">​<strong>2. <div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">​<strong>3. Compare three candidates.</strong> Pick the part in your design with the most realistic alternatives. Build a weighted matrix with at least five criteria from your spec and B3. Mark any disqualifying zero.</div>

<div style="background:linear-gradient(90deg,#dbeafe 0%,#ffffff 100%);border-left:6px solid #1e3a8a;border-radius:6px;padding:10px 16px;margin:34px 0 16px 0;font-size:22px;font-weight:700;color:#1e3a8a;">Check Your Understanding</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">​<strong>1.</strong> A chip's datasheet lists VDD absolute maximum as 2.2 V and recommended VDD as 1.7–2.0 V. A student plans to run it at 2.1 V "because it's under the maximum". What is wrong?</div>

<ul style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;padding-left:22px;"><li style="margin:6px 0;">A. Nothing; 2.1 V is below 2.2 V.</li><li style="margin:6px 0;">B. The absolute maximum is a damage limit, not an operating point. At 2.1 V the chip is outside its guaranteed range, so its behaviour is not specified.</li><li style="margin:6px 0;">C. The chip will be damaged immediately.</li><li style="margin:6px 0;">D. The chip needs exactly 1.8 V or it will not start.</li></ul>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">Answer</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">​<strong>B.</strong> Operate within the recommended range; the absolute maximum only says where damage may begin. <strong>A</strong> confuses "not broken" with "working as specified". <strong>C</strong> overstates it; 2.1 V is below the damage limit, but that guarantees nothing about correct operation. <strong>D</strong> is too strict; the whole 1.7–2.0 V range is specified.</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">​<strong>2.</strong> Two MAX30102 modules from different sellers use the same chip. One works on a shared 3.3 V I²C bus; the other drags the bus to 1.8 V. Where would you find the difference?</div>

<ul style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;padding-left:22px;"><li style="margin:6px 0;">A. In the MAX30102 datasheet</li><li style="margin:6px 0;">B. In the I²C specification</li><li style="margin:6px 0;">C. In the module's own schematic, or by measuring its pull-up voltage</li><li style="margin:6px 0;">D. In the microcontroller's datasheet</li></ul>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">Answer</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">​<strong>C.</strong> The chip datasheet describes only the chip. Pull-up voltage is the module maker's choice, visible only in its schematic or by measurement. <strong>A</strong> is identical for both modules. <strong>B</strong> defines the bus, not how any module is built. <strong>D</strong> describes the other end of the bus.</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">​<strong>3.</strong> A bare sensor chip costs about ₹1,300 at quantity 1, while a module carrying the same chip costs ₹179. For a 10-unit hand-soldered build, which is the best reason to choose the module?</div>

<ul style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;padding-left:22px;"><li style="margin:6px 0;">A. Modules are always higher quality.</li><li style="margin:6px 0;">B. It is cheaper at this quantity, hand solderable, and already provides the second supply voltage the chip needs.</li><li style="margin:6px 0;">C. Bare chips cannot be bought in India.</li><li style="margin:6px 0;">D. The module has a longer lifecycle guarantee.</li></ul>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">Answer</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">​<strong>B</strong> ties the choice to the quantity, the assembly method and the application circuit. <strong>A</strong> is false; module quality varies widely, as the reference watch's two modules show. <strong>C</strong> is false; the bare chip is stocked by distributors that ship to India. <strong>D</strong> is backwards: a marketplace module usually has no lifecycle guarantee, while the chip has a published status.</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">​<strong>4.</strong> In a weighted matrix, candidate X totals 20 but scores 0 on "works at 3.3 V" (weight 3). Candidate Y totals 17 and scores 2 on every critical criterion. What should the matrix conclude?</div>

<ul style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;padding-left:22px;"><li style="margin:6px 0;">A. X wins, because 20 &gt; 17.</li><li style="margin:6px 0;">B. The weights must be wrong, so start again.</li><li style="margin:6px 0;">C. Choose both and decide later.</li><li style="margin:6px 0;">D. Y wins: X fails a critical criterion, so it is disqualified.</li></ul>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">Answer</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">​<strong>D.</strong> A part that cannot work in your design cannot be rescued by scoring well elsewhere. <strong>A</strong> trusts the sum over the reason for the matrix. <strong>B</strong> may be worth checking, but a disqualifying zero is not evidence of bad weights. <strong>C</strong> delays the decision without adding information.</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">​<strong>5.</strong> On the day a BOM is checked, three of four modules show "out of stock" at one shop. What is the best response?</div>

<ul style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;padding-left:22px;"><li style="margin:6px 0;">A. Record the stock status and date, and find a second source for each part.</li><li style="margin:6px 0;">B. Wait for restock.</li><li style="margin:6px 0;">C. Remove those parts from the design.</li><li style="margin:6px 0;">D. Stock does not matter at the design stage.</li></ul>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">Answer</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">​<strong>A.</strong> Stock changes daily. Recording it with a date, plus a second source, makes the BOM useful to whoever builds it. <strong>B</strong> may leave the build waiting indefinitely. <strong>C</strong> overreacts to a temporary state. <strong>D</strong> is wrong: a design that cannot be bought cannot be built.</div>
