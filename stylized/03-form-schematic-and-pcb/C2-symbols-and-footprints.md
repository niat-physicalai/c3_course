<div style="background:linear-gradient(90deg,#dbeafe 0%,#ffffff 100%);border-left:6px solid #1e3a8a;border-radius:6px;padding:10px 16px;margin:34px 0 16px 0;font-size:22px;font-weight:700;color:#1e3a8a;">Drawing, Sourcing and Checking the Parts KiCad Does Not Know</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">​<strong>Course:</strong> C3 — From Problem Statement to Manufacturable Design <strong>Module:</strong> 3 — Form Factor, Schematic and PCB <strong>Time:</strong> ~1 hour · <strong>You will produce:</strong> at least one symbol and one footprint you drew yourself, and a footprint verification checklist covering every part on your board</div>

---

<div style="background:linear-gradient(90deg,#dbeafe 0%,#ffffff 100%);border-left:6px solid #1e3a8a;border-radius:6px;padding:10px 16px;margin:34px 0 16px 0;font-size:22px;font-weight:700;color:#1e3a8a;">The Part You Need Is Not in the Library</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">You press <strong>A</strong> in KiCad and type "MAX30102 module". Nothing. "MPU-6050 breakout", "SSD1306 OLED 4-pin": nothing useful. KiCad's libraries are full of chips, but the modules you chose in B4 are small boards made by many sellers, and almost nobody has drawn them properly.</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">esp\_watch hit this on every module. None of its three modules had a usable symbol, and the MAX30102 module had no footprint, so the author drew them. This unit shows how, using KiCad 10's <strong>Symbol Editor</strong> and <strong>Footprint Editor</strong>, and how to check the symbols and footprints you download instead. A wrong symbol is caught by ERC only if you typed its pins honestly. A wrong footprint is caught by nothing until the board arrives.</div>

<div style="background:linear-gradient(90deg,#dbeafe 0%,#ffffff 100%);border-left:6px solid #1e3a8a;border-radius:6px;padding:10px 16px;margin:34px 0 16px 0;font-size:22px;font-weight:700;color:#1e3a8a;">What You Will Be Able to Do After This Reading</div>

<ul style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;padding-left:22px;"><li style="margin:6px 0;">​<strong>Draw</strong> a module symbol with correct pin numbers, names and electrical types, in a project library.</li><li style="margin:6px 0;">​<strong>Decide</strong>, for each part, whether to draw, source or verify its footprint.</li><li style="margin:6px 0;">​<strong>Draw</strong> a footprint in the Footprint Editor, on the right layers, from a drawing or a calibrated photo.</li><li style="margin:6px 0;">​<strong>Verify</strong> any footprint with four measurements, and record it in a checklist.</li></ul>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">The <strong>number</strong> must match the footprint's pad number exactly: it is the only link between the schematic and the copper. The <strong>name</strong> is for people. The <strong>type</strong> is for ERC.</div>

<div style="background:linear-gradient(90deg,#dbeafe 0%,#ffffff 100%);border-left:6px solid #1e3a8a;border-radius:6px;padding:10px 16px;margin:34px 0 16px 0;font-size:22px;font-weight:700;color:#1e3a8a;">Pin Numbers Come From the Part You Will Solder</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">For a chip, pin numbers come from the datasheet. For a <strong>module</strong>, they come from the module itself: its header or edge pads, numbered in order from the end marked "1" or with a square pad. Two modules built around the same chip can have entirely different pin orders.</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">That is why esp\_watch's symbols were drawn for the modules, not the chips. The MAX30102 chip has 14 pads underneath it; the black MAX30102 module has 8 edge pads. A symbol copied from the chip's datasheet would have the wrong number of pins in the wrong order.</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">The same applies to microcontrollers. The bare ESP32-C3 chip has pins for a crystal, external flash and an antenna; the XIAO module has none of these on its edge, because they are inside. Use the module's symbol, not the chip's.</div>

<div style="background:linear-gradient(90deg,#dbeafe 0%,#ffffff 100%);border-left:6px solid #1e3a8a;border-radius:6px;padding:10px 16px;margin:34px 0 16px 0;font-size:22px;font-weight:700;color:#1e3a8a;">Electrical Types, and Why ERC Needs Them</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">ERC cannot see voltages. It only knows each pin's <strong>electrical type</strong>, and checks that connected pins make sense together [1]:</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">Two rules matter most. A <strong>power input</strong> with no power output on its net is an ERC error; C1 showed the PWR\_FLAG fix. And <strong>open-collector</strong> outputs may share a net, while ordinary outputs may not.</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">esp\_watch's three module symbols type <strong>every pin as input</strong>, including VCC, GND and SDA. The board works, because copper does not care about types. But ERC can no longer tell a supply pin from a signal: it cannot warn that a module's VCC is unpowered, or that two outputs share a net. For your own symbols, type each pin by what it does.</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">The opposite shortcut, setting every pin to passive "to make the errors go away", does the same damage. The errors go, and so does every check ERC could have made.</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">​<strong>Teaching model:</strong> I²C lets a peripheral hold SCL low to pause the controller, so some libraries type SCL as bidirectional. Either is defensible; write down which you chose.</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">Many GY-521 modules print their pins the other way round (VCC first). Neither order is wrong. What matters is that the symbol, the footprint and the module you will solder all count from the same end. Check yours against its silkscreen.</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">​<strong>Check.</strong> Place the symbol, connect VCC to +3V3, GND to ground, SDA and SCL to the bus and AD0 to ground, and put no-connect flags on INT, XCL and XDA. Run ERC. The symbol's pins produce no errors of their own.</div>

<div style="background:linear-gradient(90deg,#dbeafe 0%,#ffffff 100%);border-left:6px solid #1e3a8a;border-radius:6px;padding:10px 16px;margin:34px 0 16px 0;font-size:22px;font-weight:700;color:#1e3a8a;">How To: Draw a Symbol in the Symbol Editor</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">​<strong>Step 1: Open the Symbol Editor</strong> from the Project Manager or the schematic's top toolbar.</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">​<strong>Step 2: Create a project library.</strong> Choose <strong>File → New Library</strong>, pick <strong>Project</strong> (not Global), and save it in your project folder. A project library travels with the project, so anyone who opens it, including you on another laptop, gets the same symbols.</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">​<strong>Step 3: Create the symbol.</strong> Right-click the library and choose <strong>New Symbol</strong>. Give it a name that says exactly what it is (MPU6050\_Module\_GY521, not MPU), and set the reference designator to U.</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">​<strong>Step 4: Add the pins</strong> (<strong>P</strong>). For each pin, set its number, name and electrical type, then click to place it. Keep the default 2.54 mm (100 mil) grid, so wires in the schematic snap onto the pins.</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:14px 0 8px 0;">​<strong>Step 5: Draw the body</strong> with the rectangle tool, and lay the pins out for reading: power at the top and bottom, signals that go to the microcontroller on the left, the rest on the right.</div>

```text
                  VCC (8)
                    │
              ┌─────┴─────┐
    SCL (6) ──┤           ├── XDA (4)
    SDA (5) ──┤  MPU-6050 ├── XCL (3)
    INT (1) ──┤  module   │
    AD0 (2) ──┤           │
              └─────┬─────┘
                    │
                  GND (7)
```

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">​<strong>Step 6: Fill in the fields.</strong> Value (the part name) and, once its footprint exists, the Footprint field. Linking it now saves a mistake later.</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">​<strong>Step 7: Check every pin against your source, then save.</strong> Tick number, name and type for each pin before you place the symbol anywhere.</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">esp\_watch keeps its hand-drawn symbols in a project library, <a href="https://github.com/niat-physicalai/esp_watch/blob/main/pcb/esp_Watch/symbol.kicad_sym">pcb/esp\_Watch/symbol.kicad\_sym</a>.</div>

<div style="background:linear-gradient(90deg,#dbeafe 0%,#ffffff 100%);border-left:6px solid #1e3a8a;border-radius:6px;padding:10px 16px;margin:34px 0 16px 0;font-size:22px;font-weight:700;color:#1e3a8a;">Downloaded Symbols Need Checking Too</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">esp\_watch's XIAO symbol and footprint were downloaded from an open-source repository [3]: useful, GPL-3.0, battery pads included. Its last commit message reads "the symbol didn't have the right reference to the footprint. Not sure if this fixes it or not". That is honest, and a warning. The symbol also types the XIAO's VCC\_3V3 pin as a power input, which is why C1's ERC needed a PWR\_FLAG on +3V3.</div>

<div style="text-align:justify;line-height:1.7;margin:10px 0;">Before using a downloaded symbol, open it in the Symbol Editor and check every pin number against the module's documentation, every type, and the footprint field. Check the licence too, if you will share or sell your design files.</div>
